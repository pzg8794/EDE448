# Module 2 Comprehensive Themed Overview

## Module

**Supporting Students with Prosocial Norms in School-Based Settings**

## Essential Question

> How can support strategies respect dignity, consent, and neurodiversity?

## Purpose of This Overview

Module 2 moves from interpretation to design. Module 1 established that developmental differences are understood through labels, stories, environments, institutions, family knowledge, lived experience, and whose voice is treated as credible. Module 2 asks what educators do with those interpretations. Do support systems increase access and agency, or do they make a student easier to control?

This overview connects the Module 2 readings with:

- Module 1's diagnosis, neurodiversity, representation, sensory, and inclusion work
- Piter's Pine Brook teaching placement
- current EDU486 hands-on camp planning and field reflection
- the Puzzle Plan and EQUITAS access-to-agency framework

## Puzzle Plan and EQUITAS

The **Puzzle Plan** is Piter's interdisciplinary framework for combining lived experience, education, data, and research to understand complex problems more fully. **EQUITAS** is the equity-focused lens within that framework; it examines whose knowledge is included, what context is missing, and where power or bias may shape decisions.

Applied to behavior support, the framework asks educators to resist a single-piece explanation. A diagnosis, incident, checklist score, observation, or adult interpretation cannot explain the whole learner. The relevant system may include:

- communication access
- sensory and bodymind conditions
- language and culture
- task design and rigor
- tool and technology access
- transitions and predictability
- peers and adult positioning
- fatigue, pain, trauma, or chronic illness
- family and community knowledge
- student preferences and self-report
- adult responses and implementation fidelity

## Recommended Source Set for the Current Drafts

### Core Read 1: Dunlap et al. (2019)

*Prevent-Teach-Reinforce* provides the required method. Its five steps are teaming and goal setting, data collection, functional behavioral assessment, intervention planning, and progress monitoring. Appendix 4.1 organizes inquiry through Prevent, Teach, and Reinforce.

The method is strongest when:

- goals are socially meaningful
- behavior is defined observably
- hypotheses remain provisional
- environmental conditions and adult responses are part of the data
- the replacement route serves the same need
- reinforcement makes communication and participation effective
- student and family knowledge shape the plan
- outcomes include quality of life, dignity, access, and belonging

### Core Read 2: Gardner (2017)

Gardner's report centers first-hand perspectives from autistic people and people with developmental disabilities. Participants emphasize autonomy, consent, trauma-sensitive care, cultural competence, inclusion, self-advocacy, and support that helps a person understand and navigate disability.

This source prevents "evidence-based" from becoming the end of the ethical discussion. A support may change observable behavior while producing masking, shame, hidden harm, damaged boundaries, or distrust. Lived experience reveals outcomes that a behavior-frequency graph may not capture.

### Core Read 3: Jorgensen (2018), Communicative Competence

Jorgensen argues that presuming competence is necessary but not sufficient. Educators must construct competence through age-respectful instruction, AAC and other communication routes, collaboration, accessible materials, and authentic participation in the general curriculum.

Communication must be available all day for:

- academic thinking
- information exchange
- social closeness
- questions and comments
- needs and preferences
- refusal, boundaries, and self-advocacy

### Supplementary: Dunlap, Strain, and Fox (2012)

This chapter situates behavior intervention inside positive behavior support. It emphasizes quality of life, prevention, social and ecological validity, collaboration, systems change, stakeholder participation, engagement, implementation fidelity, and data-based adjustment. It is not the required 2019 PTR book, but it explains the philosophical commitments that make PTR more than a consequence system.

### Supplementary: Jorgensen (2018), Social Relationships

Authentic inclusion requires reciprocal relationships, not only physical placement or an assigned buddy. Students need shared experiences, communication, age-appropriate participation, and opportunities to contribute. Peer support should not permanently cast a disabled student as dependent.

### Supplementary: Zacarian and Silverstone (2015)

Classroom support begins before the first problem. Teachers can learn about strengths, languages, accommodations, sensory and physical access, family communication, prior supports, and community context in time for that knowledge to shape the room and routines.

### Supplementary Media: Edutopia

The classroom-management session usefully reframes management as instructional orchestration, predictability, relationships, clear sequencing, and transitions. It also requires critical reading. EQUITAS asks educators to challenge examples that move too quickly toward punishment or interpret a multilingual learner without adequately examining language, culture, access, or power.

## Cross-Reading Themes

### 1. Positive must describe the student's outcome

A support is not positive simply because it uses praise or reduces a visible behavior. Across the sources, stronger outcomes include:

- increased communication
- safer self-advocacy
- meaningful participation
- stronger relationships
- access to curriculum
- reduced overload
- quality of life
- belonging
- the ability to repair and return

### 2. Behavior is information, not a complete explanation

Behavior may communicate access, avoidance, pain, regulation, uncertainty, social need, sensory overload, or a learned response to what adults do. The educator's task is not to romanticize every behavior or ignore safety. It is to define what occurred, examine conditions, ask the learner and team, form a hypothesis, change supports, and gather new evidence.

### 3. Consent includes the right to communicate no

Consent in school can include:

- knowing what is happening and why
- choosing among meaningful routes
- refusing unnecessary touch, eye contact, disclosure, or public performance
- requesting help, more time, a break, or another role
- returning without shame
- participating in support decisions

Safety may require adult action, but safety must not become a vague justification for suppressing harmless neurodivergent behavior.

### 4. Communication is part of every behavior plan

A replacement behavior should not be chosen only because adults prefer it. It must be available under stress and meet the same need. Speech, gesture, AAC, pointing, typing, drawing, familiar tools, and partner-supported communication can all be valid.

### 5. Prevention is classroom architecture

Prevention includes:

- visual agendas and sequences
- short, clear directions
- modeled starting points
- predictable transitions
- sensory and movement options
- familiar supports
- multiple participation routes
- meaningful roles
- proactive check-ins
- language access
- family partnership

### 6. Reinforcement must not become masking pressure

Reinforcement should strengthen communication, contribution, repair, persistence, re-entry, and agency. Quietness, stillness, eye contact, public speaking, or speed should not be universal success criteria.

### 7. Data must include the system

Useful evidence includes:

- what the task required
- what support was offered
- whether the support was accessible
- what adults and peers did
- what the student communicated
- what changed after redesign
- plan fidelity
- student and family evaluation
- outcomes beyond behavior reduction

## Placement Evidence: Familiar Laptop and Blocks

During Piter's Pine Brook placement, a learner protested when an adult attempted to remove a familiar laptop during a transition to building with blocks. Piter allowed the laptop and hands-on materials to coexist. The learner began building and remained connected.

This event supports several conclusions:

- forced removal was not necessary for participation
- the familiar tool may have supported predictability, confidence, regulation, communication, or preference
- the adult's transition demand belongs in the evidence
- response to support can reveal competence

It does **not** establish:

- a diagnosis
- a fixed behavioral function
- a universal preference
- what the learner felt internally

That distinction is the basis of the [PTR Appendix 4.1 completion notes](../ptr-appendix-4-1-completion-notes.md).

## EDU486 Hands-On Practice Bridge

The Invisible Invaders camp work provides current evidence that these principles apply beyond technology lessons.

Youth feedback on July 21 led to:

- short demonstrations
- one direction at a time
- visible sequences
- rapid entry into activity
- speaking, drawing, pointing, writing, dictation, photography, partner, pass, and re-entry routes
- proactive check-ins
- meaningful extensions
- recognition for noticing, explaining, revising, and helping rather than speed

The July 24 field reflection found:

- youth-generated sampling sites and scientific questions
- meaningful roles with materials and magnification
- a more adult-directed experience than intended
- compressed closure
- incomplete visibility of response choices
- a timed count that could still privilege speed
- recordings that preserved some evidence but could not establish every learner's understanding, desire, identity, or need

This is the Puzzle Plan in practice: plan from multiple evidence sources, observe what the environment makes possible, name what the data cannot show, and redesign before blaming the learner.

## Intersectional Reading

Behavior support is never applied to disability alone. Gardner's cultural-competence section and Piter's lived experience require attention to:

- race and racialized discipline
- bilingual and multilingual communication
- culture and family meaning
- gender and diagnostic stereotypes
- poverty and uneven access to services
- chronic illness, pain, fatigue, and fluctuating energy
- trauma and trust
- technology access and prior experience

EQUITAS asks whether the same behavior is interpreted differently depending on who displays it and whether access requires a student to disclose private pain.

Keep the response concise: positive support is judged by what it gives students; behavior data must include the environment; future practice will use visible access, communication, student/family goals, and richer outcomes.

## Final Themed Synthesis

Module 2 does not ask educators to choose between structure and freedom, safety and consent, or rigor and inclusion. It asks them to design structure that increases freedom, protect safety without erasing communication, and preserve rigor by removing barriers that hide thinking.

Positive support is only positive when the student has more agency, communication, access, relationship, and dignity after the intervention than before it.
