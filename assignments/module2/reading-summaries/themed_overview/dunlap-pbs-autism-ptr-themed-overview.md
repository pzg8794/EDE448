# Dunlap Positive Behavior Support and Prevent-Teach-Reinforce - Themed Overview

## Source Identity and Important Distinction

Two related but different Dunlap sources support this module:

- **Required source:** Dunlap et al. (2019), *Prevent-Teach-Reinforce: The School-Based Model of Individualized Positive Behavior Support* (2nd ed.). The course requires at least one chapter and review of its resources.
- **Local supplemental PDF:** Dunlap, Strain, and Fox (2012), "Positive Behavior Support and Young People with Autism: Strategies of Prevention and Intervention," in *Handbook of Implementation Science for Psychology in Education*.

The local file `Module2/Read/Dunlap PBS and Autism.pdf` is the 2012 chapter, not the required 2019 PTR book. It should strengthen the analysis but should not be represented as the assigned PTR chapter.

## Required PTR Source

The publisher describes PTR as a five-step school-based process:

1. teaming and goal setting
2. data collection
3. functional behavioral assessment
4. behavior-intervention planning
5. progress monitoring and data-based decision making

For the current assignment package, the selected resource is **Appendix 4.1, PTR Functional Behavioral Assessment Checklist**. The form organizes evidence through Prevent, Teach, and Reinforce components and makes it possible to examine antecedents, possible function, adult responses, replacement skills, and meaningful reinforcement.

## What the 2012 PBS Chapter Adds

The 2012 chapter places individualized intervention inside a broader positive behavior support framework. Its distinguishing commitments include:

- quality-of-life improvement
- longitudinal and functional outcomes
- ecological validity
- collaboration with principal stakeholders
- social validity of goals and procedures
- systems change and multicomponent support
- prevention
- multiple methods and theoretical perspectives

The chapter traces PBS partly to a rejection of consequence-heavy and aversive behavior management. It frames serious behavior as potentially functional and communicative, emphasizes antecedent and contextual analysis, and describes universal, secondary, and individualized prevention. It also stresses implementation fidelity, feasible data collection, family systems, engagement across everyday routines, and goals that matter in the person's life.

## Dignity, Consent, and Neurodiversity

PTR can respect dignity when the target is meaningful access or safety rather than adult preference. Prevention should reduce barriers before crisis. Teaching should create a functionally useful communication, coping, academic, or social route. Reinforcement should make that route effective for the learner.

The model can still be misused if adults:

- define harmless difference as the problem
- choose goals without student or family influence
- infer function from one event
- measure only the absence of behavior
- ignore adult implementation and environmental conditions
- reward masking, surrender, or quietness instead of communication and participation

Consent requires the team to treat refusal, hesitation, and other communication as evidence. Safety remains an adult responsibility, but it must not become a blanket justification for controlling neurodivergent movement, communication, or regulation.

## Puzzle Plan and EQUITAS

The Puzzle Plan treats behavior as one piece in a larger relationship among the learner, task, tools, sensory conditions, language, culture, peers, family knowledge, and adult response. EQUITAS asks whose interpretation becomes the official function, whose preferred outcome counts as "socially valid," and whether the plan increases agency.

This lens expands PTR data beyond "What did the student do?" to include:

- What did the environment demand?
- Which communication routes were available?
- What did the adult remove, add, or change?
- What did the student indicate?
- What happened when support changed?
- Did the outcome improve dignity, access, safety, belonging, or quality of life?

## Placement and Camp Connections

The Pine Brook laptop-and-blocks event is a useful PTR case because participation increased after an adult stopped requiring abrupt removal of a familiar tool. The event supports a tentative hypothesis and an environmental redesign; it does not establish a fixed function.

The EDU486 camp work shows PTR at a broader level. Visible sequences, short directions, participation choices, proactive help, sensory and lower-energy routes, and protected closure are prevention supports. Pointing, drawing, dictating, photographing, passing, and re-entering are teachable communication routes. Recognition should strengthen noticing, revising, helping, and explaining rather than speed.

## Final Themed Takeaway

PTR is strongest when it turns behavior support into collaborative inquiry: prevent unnecessary barriers, teach a communication or participation route that meets the same need, reinforce that route because it works, and revise the plan when the student's life does not improve.
