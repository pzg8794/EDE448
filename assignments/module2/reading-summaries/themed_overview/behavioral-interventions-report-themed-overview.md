## What This Source Is

This is not a generic evidence-ranking report. It centers first-hand perspectives from ten autistic people and people with related developmental disabilities who discussed experiences with behavioral interventions. Participants contributed through phone, in-person conversation, online chat, and an online questionnaire. Those communication routes are not ranked; the value of the evidence lies in what participants identify about outcomes, harm, autonomy, and care.

## Central Findings

Participants emphasized six connected priorities:

1. promote meaningful outcomes
2. prevent harm
3. protect autonomy and consent
4. support inclusion and self-advocacy
5. use trauma-sensitive care
6. practice cultural competence

The report distinguishes skills that help a person understand and navigate their disability from interventions designed mainly to make someone appear less autistic. Participants valued sensory regulation, self-care, mentoring, accessibility, boundaries, and meaningful social inclusion. They described harm when harmless stimming was suppressed, compliance was taught without context, or outward behavior was changed while overload, stress, and communication needs remained unaddressed.

## Dignity

Dignity requires goals that matter to the person. A quieter or more typical-looking student is not sufficient evidence of a positive outcome. The report identifies hidden outcomes that behavior counts may miss:

- shame and damaged self-esteem
- masking and indistinguishability pressure
- hidden or more harmful replacement regulation
- trauma from restraint, punishment, or forced exposure
- exclusion from community
- reduced trust in adults and services

This source makes lived experience part of the evidence base rather than a story added after professional analysis.

## Consent and Power

Participants repeatedly connect behavioral treatment to power. Teaching a child that adults may override every "no" can weaken later boundaries and self-protection. The report supports:

- offering meaningful choices
- teaching refusal and self-advocacy
- explaining why a skill or norm matters
- treating the professional as a guide rather than unquestioned authority
- adapting a support when the person communicates that it is harmful

Consent does not eliminate adult responsibility for safety. It changes how adults interpret communication and how much power the learner has inside the plan.

## Neurodiversity and Cultural Competence

The report challenges a narrow public image of autism as white, male, and economically privileged. Participants note that disability may be missed or moralized differently across race, gender, class, housing status, and access to services. An EQUITAS reading asks who is diagnosed, who is punished, who is believed, and whose behavior is interpreted as disability rather than bad character.

## Puzzle Plan and EQUITAS

The Puzzle Plan combines lived experience, education, data, and research. Gardner's report shows why those evidence types must remain connected. A support team may record frequency and intensity while missing trauma, masking, or bodily autonomy. EQUITAS asks whether the person receiving the intervention has meaningful influence over the goal, method, and definition of success.

## Placement and Camp Connections

The Pine Brook laptop-and-blocks event reflects the report's warning against suppressing a visible behavior before understanding its purpose. Allowing a familiar tool to remain created participation without forcing surrender.

In EDU486 camp work, no learner should have to disclose a diagnosis, health history, or trauma to receive a seated route, processing time, another communication mode, or permission to pass and re-enter. Those access supports protect autonomy without reducing scientific rigor.

## Final Themed Takeaway

Behavioral support cannot be evaluated only by what adults can see. Autistic and disabled people must help define meaningful outcomes, acceptable methods, and the difference between support and enforced performance.
