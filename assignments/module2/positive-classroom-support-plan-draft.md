## Plan Purpose

My plan is designed for an inclusive, technology-rich upper-elementary or middle school classroom in which students may differ in communication, sensory processing, movement, executive functioning, language, culture, disability, chronic illness, prior technology access, energy, and comfort with group work. The goal is not to make every student look regulated, attentive, or social in the same way. The goal is to create a classroom where rigorous learning is accessible, expectations are understandable, communication is supported, students can exercise agency, and harm is addressed without confusing safety with conformity.

This plan uses the Puzzle Plan and EQUITAS as organizing frameworks. The Puzzle Plan is my interdisciplinary framework for combining lived experience, education, data, and research to understand complex problems more fully. EQUITAS is the equity-focused lens within that framework; it asks whose knowledge is included, what context is missing, and where power or bias may shape decisions. Together, they require me to study relationships among the learner, task, tools, environment, peers, adults, culture, language, and prior experience before explaining the learner as the problem.

## 1. Commitments That Define "Positive"

I will judge the plan by whether it increases:

- dignity and bodily autonomy
- safety without unnecessary coercion
- communication and self-advocacy
- access to rigorous learning
- meaningful choice and consent
- regulation without masking pressure
- reciprocal relationships and belonging
- repair after conflict
- student and family influence
- adult accountability and willingness to redesign

Dunlap, Strain, and Fox (2012) connect positive behavior support to quality of life, stakeholder collaboration, social validity, ecological validity, prevention, and systems change. Gardner's (2017) first-hand report adds a crucial test: a strategy is not positive if it produces surface-level conformity through shame, trauma, suppression of harmless regulation, or weakened boundaries. A reduction in visible behavior is one outcome, not the whole definition of success.

## 2. Learn Before Problems Emerge

Before the school year begins, I will learn about students as people and prepare the environment. Zacarian and Silverstone (2015) show that classroom-community planning begins with questions about accommodations, language, physical access, sensory and movement needs, family communication, prior supports, strengths, and important relationships. I will review available IEPs and support plans, but I will not treat them as complete biographies.

My beginning-of-year process will include:

- learning and correctly pronouncing each student's chosen name
- identifying the languages students and families use for communication
- confirming AAC, assistive technology, sensory, mobility, health, and access supports
- asking students what helps them begin, pause, communicate, collaborate, and recover
- inviting families to share strengths, interests, routines, successful supports, and concerns
- coordinating with special educators, paraprofessionals, related-service providers, and prior teachers
- checking the room for mobility, visibility, sound, lighting, seating, and technology barriers
- preparing supports before a student must fail publicly to receive them

Family and student knowledge will be invited, not extracted. No student should have to disclose a diagnosis, trauma, body history, poverty, culture, or family hardship to prove that an access route is needed.

## 3. Co-Create a Small Set of Community Commitments

The class will develop a short set of values that protect everyone's right to learn and belong:

- We communicate needs and boundaries.
- We make room for different bodies, brains, languages, and communication styles.
- We help without taking over.
- We use materials, tools, and space safely.
- We repair harm and improve the system.

Students will examine what each commitment means during a mini-lesson, hands-on investigation, digital task, group project, transition, break, and closure. Examples and nonexamples will be modeled without singling out a student. This preserves structure while making classroom culture a shared problem to solve rather than a list of adult commands.

## 4. Make the Learning Route Visible

Every lesson will use a predictable sequence:

1. preview the purpose and full route
2. connect to prior knowledge or a real question
3. model one starting move
4. give one direction at a time
5. support investigation or practice
6. pause to notice, explain, or revise
7. close with more than one response route
8. preview what comes next

Students will be able to see what is required, what can be chosen, and what can wait. A **Must Do / Can Do / Skip Today** structure can support pacing without lowering the learning goal. Visible agendas, task cards, completed examples, timers used as information rather than threats, transition warnings, and clear stopping points reduce hidden language and executive-function demands.

The Edutopia classroom-management session describes management as orchestration, instructional clarity, predictability, and relationship rather than control. That idea fits my experience: many "management problems" begin when students cannot see the sequence, understand the first step, access the tool, or predict the transition.

## 5. Design Multiple Routes Into the Same Rigor

Students will have meaningful ways to engage and demonstrate competence without one posture, pace, or communication mode becoming the hidden standard. Depending on the task, routes may include:

- speaking, typing, writing, drawing, pointing, gesture, AAC, dictation, or private audio
- standing, sitting, moving, observing first, or using a lower-energy route
- working independently, beside a peer, with a chosen role, or with adult support
- manipulating materials, photographing evidence, coding, labeling, mapping, modeling, explaining, or revising
- passing in the moment and re-entering through a visible next step

These are equivalent routes into thinking, not escape routes from rigor. Each option must still reveal evidence connected to the learning goal.

My EDU486 camp work has tested this principle in a hands-on setting. Youth asked for short explanations and a chance to begin quickly. On our first field day, participants observed, chose sampling locations, scooped and labeled sand, counted, used magnification, asked questions, and explained ideas. Our reflection also showed missing routes: closure centered oral responses, not every choice was visible, and a timed count could feel like a speed test. The lesson plan itself must remain part of the behavior-support data.

## 6. Provide Communication All Day, Every Day

Jorgensen (2018) argues that educators must actively construct communicative competence, not merely presume it. Every student needs access to communication for academic ideas, social connection, wants and needs, refusal, questions, repair, and self-advocacy.

The classroom will provide reliable ways to communicate:

- help
- more time
- show me
- I do not understand
- not yet
- no or stop
- break
- different tool, role, partner, or location
- I am ready to return
- I disagree
- I need to repair something

These messages may be expressed through speech, writing, symbols, gestures, AAC, technology, pointing, or a partner-supported route. Adults will model and respond to them consistently. A communication support that is present only during therapy or only after escalation is not dependable access.

## 7. Build Sensory and Regulation Access Into Tier 1

The room will include:

- flexible seating and posture options
- movement and lower-energy routes
- predictable sound expectations
- quieter work locations
- visual-clutter checks
- access to appropriate sensory tools
- a reset area that is not used as punishment
- transition previews and recovery time
- permission to use familiar tools when they support participation and safety

Movement, silence, scripting, looking away, or requesting a break will not automatically be treated as avoidance. Adults will ask whether the student is regulating, processing, communicating, protecting against overload, or navigating pain or fatigue. Individual supports will be added when universal design is insufficient.

## 8. Teach the Skills the Community Actually Needs

I will explicitly model and practice:

- how to begin and how to identify the first step
- how to ask for help without public embarrassment
- how to request a break and how to return
- how to refuse or negotiate safely
- how to use a visual sequence or debugging guide
- how to take a role without controlling a peer
- how to invite participation without forcing it
- how to disagree, set a boundary, and repair harm
- how to respond when another person communicates differently
- how to transition with a familiar support

Teaching will happen during calm, ordinary routines, not only after a student has difficulty. Students will understand why a skill matters rather than being told that one social performance is simply "appropriate."

## 9. Use Reinforcement for Agency, Contribution, and Repair

Reinforcement will make meaningful skills effective for the learner. I will notice and support:

- communicating a need or boundary
- using a support before distress escalates
- beginning through an accessible route
- returning after a break
- explaining thinking or uncertainty
- revising an idea with evidence
- supporting a peer with permission
- trying a different strategy
- repairing harm
- contributing quietly or outside the most visible role

I will not use public behavior charts, humiliation, removal of earned dignity, or rewards based mainly on speed, eye contact, stillness, public speaking, or quiet compliance. Praise will not be assumed to be reinforcing for every student. Student preference and natural outcomes matter: access to the task, successful communication, a valued role, choice, competence, connection, and completion with integrity.

## 10. Use PTR for Individualized Support

When universal supports are not enough, I will use the five-step PTR process described by Dunlap et al. (2019):

1. build a team and set socially meaningful goals
2. collect baseline data
3. conduct a functional behavioral assessment
4. develop a Prevent-Teach-Reinforce intervention plan
5. monitor progress, fidelity, and data-based decisions

The student will participate to the greatest accessible extent. The team will use observable definitions and distinguish observation from inference. Functional hypotheses will remain provisional. The plan will include adult actions and environmental changes, not only a replacement behavior for the student.

The placement event used in my Appendix 4.1 draft illustrates this approach. A learner protested when a familiar laptop was being removed during a transition to blocks. Allowing the laptop to remain while inviting the learner into the hands-on activity increased participation. The correct lesson is not "always give a laptop." It is to ask what role the familiar tool played, preserve what supported access, teach a reliable transition communication route, and gather more evidence before making a broad claim.

## 11. Separate Safety, Difference, and Harm

The classroom needs a clear safety plan, but not every difference is a safety problem.

- Immediate danger requires calm protective action using the least intrusive effective response.
- Harmless regulation or unconventional participation does not require extinction.
- Conflict requires accountability and repair.
- Confusion or overload requires access and communication support.
- Repeated concern requires team-based assessment, not moral labels.

After a safety event, adults will review the antecedent conditions, unmet communication need, environmental load, response used, recovery, and what must change. The student should not carry the full burden of prevention.

## 12. Repair Harm Without Erasing Context

When harm occurs, I will use a brief, accessible repair process:

1. What happened, based on observable evidence?
2. What did each person need or communicate?
3. Who or what was affected?
4. What is needed for safety and repair now?
5. What skill, support, or environmental change is needed next time?
6. How will the student re-enter the community?

Context does not erase accountability. Accountability without context, however, often repeats the same harm. Repair may use speaking, writing, drawing, visuals, mediation, or a later conversation after regulation.

## 14. Data and Review

Data will help the team learn, not simply prove that a student is the problem. I will document:

- the routine and learning demand
- what occurred before and after
- communication and participation routes available
- sensory, language, technology, peer, and transition conditions
- student communication or self-report
- support offered and whether it was actually accessible
- adult implementation fidelity
- response to the support
- participation, recovery, belonging, safety, and academic access
- whose knowledge was included in the decision

The team will ask whether the plan is feasible, acceptable, socially meaningful, and improving quality of life. If a visible behavior decreases while communication, trust, or participation also decreases, the plan is not successful.

## 15. Family and Team Partnership

Students, families, teachers, paraprofessionals, related-service providers, and other relevant team members should share knowledge and responsibility. Communication will be available in the family's language. Meetings will begin with strengths and priorities, explain data in understandable language, and distinguish evidence from hypothesis. Family expertise will not be treated as secondary to school data, and student disagreement will not be erased from the record.

## Conclusion

I want my classroom to be structured enough that students can predict and trust it, and flexible enough that they do not have to erase themselves to belong. Inclusion and rigor are the same design problem: both require me to make thinking visible, gather better evidence, and redesign barriers before explaining the learner as the problem.

A positive classroom support plan should help students communicate, participate, set boundaries, build relationships, take academic risks, and repair harm. It should also make adults more accountable for the conditions they create. I will know the plan is working when students have more access and agency, not merely when the room is quieter.
