\reviewsection{Purpose and Student Profile}

This plan supports a nonspeaking upper-elementary learner during an inclusive evidence-to-action science routine and the home--school transition that follows it. The learner uses a speech-generating AAC system with direct touch, gestures, facial expression, and object-based choices. A printed core-and-fringe board provides backup. The student enjoys visual evidence, sorting, photography, and demonstrating with materials. The support goal is reliable authorship across observation, model revision, group decision-making, sensory choices, showcase preparation, and communication with home.

The plan extends my submitted PTR paper, \textit{From Compliance to Communication}, without repeating its behavior analysis \citep{garciabautista2026ptr}. The earlier paper showed how removing an unnecessary either-or condition changed participation. This plan adds a communication system through which a learner could ask to keep a familiar tool, reject a material, request a different sequence, comment on evidence, or repair an adult's interpretation.

\reviewsection{A Multimodal Communication System}

\citet{mirenda2015aids} describes AAC as a continuously updated combination of aided and unaided techniques. ASHA also notes that AAC has no age, cognitive-score, or milestone prerequisite \citep{ashaAAC}. No mode must be abandoned to prove proficiency with another.

\reviewsection{Vocabulary Must Support More Than Requesting}

Core language includes \textit{I, you, it, want, not, more, different, stop, go, help, look, think, like, tell,} and \textit{why}. Activity-specific vocabulary includes \textit{evidence, compare, count, model, label, arrow, water, sample, smell, texture, microscope,} and \textit{showcase}. Repair language includes \textit{wait, show me, say it another way, not that, I meant something else,} and \textit{you misunderstood}.

The system must support choosing, refusing, commenting, questioning, directing a partner, expressing sensory preferences or pain, predicting, comparing, explaining, revising, joking, connecting socially, asking for help or a pause, correcting an interpretation, and deciding what message travels home.

\reviewsection{Communication Partner Plan}

Adults and peers will keep the system within reach, model language on an adult or partner board, pause long enough for processing, notice all modes, and confirm rather than assume. They will not require the student to repeat a clear gesture on the device, remove AAC as a consequence, physically prompt a message without consent, speak about the student as if absent, or treat refusal as a failed trial.

When a message is unclear, the partner will state what was noticed, offer ways to confirm or repair, and leave room for an answer adults did not anticipate: ``I saw you push the sample away. Are you saying \textit{stop}, \textit{different tool}, \textit{need space}, or something else?'' The student can answer through any reliable mode.

\citet{peckhamhardin2015behavior} emphasize that a functional communication response must be acknowledged and honored. A break message that never produces a break teaches the learner that the new pathway is unreliable.

\reviewsection{A Student-Centered Home--School Record}

The supplied Daily Communication Logs offer many formats, but several center adult ratings such as good, fair, or poor; compliance; warnings; sitting; or parent initials \citep{dailylogs2009}. I would replace that emphasis with the record below.

\reviewsection{Data, Review, Consent, and Success}

For two weeks, the team will sample the selected science routine and dismissal. Data will record communication opportunities offered, modes used, message functions, partner wait/modeling, breakdowns and repairs, device or board availability, sensory or task conditions, and whether the message changed what happened. The team will review patterns with the student and family rather than merely count device activations.

The plan will be revised if it increases prompts without increasing authorship, if the student avoids the system, if partners dominate the interaction, or if messages are acknowledged but not acted on. The success question comes from my Module 2 journal, \textit{Who Has More Power After Support?} \citep{garciabautista2026module2journal}: after support, does the student have more dignity, agency, belonging, access, and power to influence the environment? Communication support is successful when it travels with the student and allows the student's message to matter.
