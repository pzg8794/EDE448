## Purpose and Scenario

This prospective plan supports a nonspeaking upper-elementary learner during an inclusive evidence-to-action science routine and the home-school transition that follows it. The learner uses a speech-generating AAC system with direct touch and also communicates through reaching, pointing, facial expression, body movement, photographs, objects, and a printed core-and-fringe board. The student enjoys visual evidence, sorting, photography, building models, and demonstrating with materials. The support goal is reliable authorship across observation, model revision, group decisions, sensory choices, peer interaction, showcase preparation, and communication with home.

The student profile is a non-identifying assignment scenario. EDU486 camp evidence contributes routine design, sensory-access ideas, flexible participation routes, student reflection structures, and evidence-to-action planning. The profile itself is prospective and would be confirmed with the student, family, speech-language pathologist, and instructional team before implementation.

The assignment asks for a detailed description of a nonspeaking student's communication needs, specific instructional and communication strategies, implementation planning, and citations. The plan below makes each of those elements explicit. The course also emphasizes assessment of communication modes, physical and sensory access, low- and high-tech systems, choice, independence, and collaboration with students and families.

## Individual Communication Profile and Planning Assumptions

| Area | Specific Communication Need | Planning Response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reliable current modes | Direct-touch AAC, reaching/pointing, facial expression, body movement, photographs, and moving an object toward or away from a partner all carry meaning | Treat every reliable mode as communication and confirm meaning through the student's easiest available route |
| Expressive message range | Familiar choices are accessible; comments, questions, refusal, sensory messages, academic language, social language, humor, and repair need efficient access | Provide stable core vocabulary plus science, identity, sensory, social, pain, refusal, and repair vocabulary before the routine begins |
| Comprehension and receptive access | Long spoken directions and rapid changes can make the routine harder to follow | Pair speech with a visible first/next/last sequence, completed examples, photographs, shared models, and one direction at a time |
| Processing and partner pace | Rapid questions and repeated prompting can interrupt message formulation | Ask one question, model without demanding imitation, wait at least 10 seconds, and confirm the student's response before adding another prompt |
| Motor and positioning access | Direct touch is reliable when the system is stable and within reach; carrying materials can block access | Use a stable mount or nonslip surface, position the display within reach, and keep a partner board beside hands-on materials |
| Sensory access | Smell, texture, crowding, noise, and direct contact with materials may change access | Make *stop, different, too much, space, observe only, different material,* and *ready later* easy to reach; provide a non-contact route to the same learning goal |
| Literacy and visual access | Familiar symbols, photographs, and selected printed words support meaning; frequent layout changes slow navigation | Pair symbols with print, use photographs for activity referents, preserve predictable locations, and change layouts only after review with the communicator |
| Social and academic participation | The student needs routes to direct peers, disagree, ask questions, revise a model, explain evidence, joke, and choose a public or private role | Program peer names and activity vocabulary; use flexible group roles; create chances to initiate, redirect, and teach others |
| Repair and self-advocacy | Communication breakdowns can leave an adult interpretation in place | Keep *wait, not that, show me, say another way, you misunderstood,* and *something else* available across primary and backup systems |
| Home-school continuity and privacy | Adult-selected reports can erase the student's authorship or share information the student would prefer to keep private | Let the student select, revise, approve, restrict, or decline the outgoing message and invite family context back through an accessible route |

The first implementation meeting turns these assumptions into confirmed access information: what the student already communicates, with whom, through which modes and languages, under what motor and sensory conditions, which vocabulary matters at home and school, what the student wants partners to understand, and which messages the student wants to make easier. The profile changes whenever the student's own communication or family knowledge changes the team's understanding.

## Access-to-Agency Planning Path

The Puzzle Plan connects lived experience, observation, student words, artifacts, context, family knowledge, and instructional decisions so that one data point never defines the learner. EQUITAS asks whose knowledge enters the plan, what context is missing, how power shapes interpretation, and whether support expands dignity, agency, belonging, and access. In this communication plan, those questions become a concrete pathway:

| Planning Element | What Must Be Available |
| --- | --- |
| Student meaning | idea, question, refusal, joke, sensory boundary, correction, academic contribution, social connection |
| Access route | direct touch, pointing, gesture, photograph, object, partner-assisted access, printed display |
| Communication system | primary AAC device, stable low-tech backup, shared visuals, activity materials |
| Partner practice | model, wait, notice, confirm, ask before helping, return control, respond to the message |
| Consequence | the message can change pace, material, role, question, sequence, support, audience, or next action |

Communication succeeds when the student's meaning survives changes in tool, partner, place, and routine and can influence what happens next.

## Multimodal Communication System

Mirenda (2015) describes AAC as an evolving combination of aided and unaided techniques. Schlosser et al. (2014) likewise emphasize assessment, feature matching, aided input, communication functions, partners, and generalization. ASHA identifies AAC as a valid option across ages and support profiles without prerequisite cognitive or developmental milestones. Existing efficient communication modes remain valid alongside a speech-generating system.

| Component | Purpose | Access Safeguard |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary AAC device | Core language, personalized vocabulary, academic language, and speech output | Charged, positioned, and available across the full routine |
| Printed core-and-fringe board | Stable backup during charging, positioning changes, hands-on materials, or communicator preference | Preserve key vocabulary and predictable locations from the primary system when useful |
| Gestures, facial expression, body movement | Existing efficient communication | Partners notice, confirm, and respond without requiring device repetition |
| Objects, photos, models, and materials | Concrete referents for evidence, tools, activity choices, and shared meaning | Use them to support open communication as well as choices |
| Shared visual sequence | Comprehension, predictability, and transition support | Show first/next/last, changes, optional routes, and a visible re-entry point |
| Repair route | Fast clarification when a message is misunderstood | Keep *not that, wait, show me, say another way, you misunderstood,* and *something else* visible |

## Full Communicative Range

Core language includes *I, you, it, want, not, more, different, stop, go, help, look, think, like, tell, why, where,* and *when*. Activity vocabulary includes *evidence, compare, count, model, revise, sample, water, tire, litter, microscope, smell, texture, observe only, different material, policy,* and *showcase*. Social and identity vocabulary is selected with the student and family. Repair language includes *wait, not that, show me, say another way, you misunderstood, I meant something else,* and *something else*.

The system supports choosing, refusing, commenting, questioning, directing a partner, expressing pain or sensory boundaries, predicting, comparing, explaining, revising, joking, greeting, connecting socially, asking for help or more time, repairing misunderstandings, teaching another person, and deciding what information travels home. Schlosser et al. (2014) support evaluating AAC across communication functions, partners, settings, maintenance, and generalization. A request-only system would leave much of the student's academic and social authorship inaccessible.

## AAC Selection and Trial Questions

| Domain | Team Question | Evidence During Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Communicator | Which modes, languages, identities, interests, relationships, and messages matter now? | Initiations, preference, fatigue, refusal, repair, and the student's choice among available routes |
| Access | Which touch, positioning, motor, visual, hearing, sensory, and partner-assisted routes are usable? | Effort and accuracy across locations, materials, body positions, and times of day |
| Vocabulary | Are academic, social, identity, bilingual, humor, refusal, pain, sensory, and repair messages reachable? | The student communicates across several functions and can find important words without constant adult reprogramming |
| Partners | Who models, waits, notices, confirms, repairs, and responds? | Adults reduce repeated prompts, recognize multiple modes, and allow messages to affect the plan |
| Continuity | Does communication remain available in class, transitions, hands-on work, home, and device charging or failure? | High-tech, low-tech, and existing modes remain usable across routines |
| Outcome | Does communication increase learning, relationships, privacy, choice, and influence? | More self-initiated contributions, broader message functions, successful repairs, and messages that produce meaningful responses |

Norrie, Waller, and Hannah (2021) show that AAC adoption depends on training, technical support, staffing, coordination, device access, and daily context. These are implementation conditions the team must plan for from the beginning.

## Communication Opportunity Matrix

Downing, Hanreddy, and Peckham-Hardin (2015) support embedding communication opportunities across ordinary classroom routines and peer interactions. The camp evidence strengthens the design because participation already included demonstrating, pointing, writing, photographing, partnering, observing, pausing, and returning.

| Routine | Student Can Communicate | Partner Action | Evidence of Access |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Arrival and preview | greet, ask what comes next, choose or decline a first role | Review visual sequence; model available words; leave AAC within reach | Student accesses the sequence and makes, changes, or declines a choice |
| Observe and collect | direct attention, choose tool, comment, reject smell/texture, ask for space | Keep device/board near materials; offer direct-contact and non-contact routes | Mode, message, environmental condition, and adult response are recorded |
| Compare evidence | same/different, more/less, prediction, question, disagreement | Wait; accept pointing, symbols, photos, objects, gesture, or device output | Student contributes evidence and questions through a reliable mode |
| Revise model | add/remove, label, arrow, disagree, explain, ask for clarification | Give control of materials or a partner-directed role | Student's contribution changes the shared model |
| Group decision | vote, pass, propose another option, request time, identify an access barrier | Make vote, write-in, pass, and return routes visible | Decision record preserves the student's authored position |
| Showcase | demonstrate, point, answer, ask, hold evidence, partner, observe, change role | Rehearse the chosen role and keep a private or partner-supported route available | Student chooses how to participate and can revise the role |
| Dismissal/home bridge | choose a message, question, contribution, barrier, or change request for home | Co-author the record and let the student review what will travel | Student approves, revises, restricts, or declines the outgoing message |

## Flexible Peer Roles and Group Participation

Peer interaction creates communication opportunities when the student has meaningful work and control over the route. Possible roles include starter/modeler, evidence finder, recorder/mapper, connector/questioner, reviser/debugger, materials/access lead, presenter/demonstrator, and observer/returner. Roles remain changeable. Each role can be completed through AAC, gesture, pointing, writing, photographs, direct material use, partner direction, observation, or another reliable mode. An observer/returner role always includes a visible step back into substantive group work.

Peers learn four partner actions: **invite, wait, ask, and respect**. They ask before helping, wait for a response, keep communication tools with their owner, accept refusal, and return control after assistance. Downing et al. (2015) support treating peer interaction as part of the communicative environment, with authentic opportunities to initiate, repair, disagree, and build relationships.

## Communication Partner Plan

Adults and peers will:

- keep the primary system and backup within reach across the routine;
- model language on an adult or partner display without requiring imitation;
- ask one question and protect processing time;
- recognize AAC, gesture, facial expression, body movement, pointing, objects, photographs, and writing as valid communication;
- state what they noticed and ask the student to confirm when meaning is unclear;
- ask before touching the student's device, materials, body, or workspace;
- return control immediately after assistance;
- address the student directly and protect private communication;
- make refusal, correction, disagreement, humor, pain, and sensory boundaries consequential;
- record partner barriers and implementation failures alongside student communication data.

When a message is unclear, a partner can say: “I saw you push the sample away. Are you saying *stop*, *different tool*, *need space*, or something else?” The student can confirm or repair through any reliable mode. Peckham-Hardin (2015) explains that a functional communication response must produce a meaningful outcome. A message such as *break*, *stop*, or *different* becomes reliable when partners recognize it and respond consistently.

## Low-Tech Continuity Plan

The printed board preserves the student's message range during charging, hands-on materials, positioning changes, technology problems, or moments when another route is easier. Key core, sensory, academic, and repair vocabulary stays in predictable locations when that supports access. Copies will be kept at the student's primary work area, in the science activity materials, and with the home-school communication materials. The teacher and speech-language pathologist jointly maintain vocabulary and layout; classroom staff check availability at the beginning of the routine; the student identifies missing or unwanted vocabulary during review.

Gesture, writing, objects, photographs, and other efficient routes remain available alongside the printed board. Rejection of the paper display becomes information about access, representation, effort, or preference. The team responds by revising the system with the communicator.

## Student-Centered Home-School Record

The supplied *Daily Communication Logs* packet demonstrates many school-home formats, including ratings, checklists, teacher comments, family comments, and parent initials. Several examples center adult judgments such as good/fair/poor days, warnings, sitting, and compliance (EDE 448 Course Materials, 2009). This plan uses a student-centered record that carries communication, context, and learner authorship across settings.

| Field | Student-Centered Entry |
| --- | --- |
| Student-authored or student-approved message to home |  |
| Communication mode(s) used |  |
| Activity and environmental conditions |  |
| What the student chose, refused, asked, commented, taught, or repaired |  |
| Support that increased access |  |
| Barrier or condition to change tomorrow |  |
| Question or unfinished idea |  |
| Family message, vocabulary, context, or question for school |  |
| Student reviewed the outgoing message | approved / changed / restricted / chose not to send |

The record gives the learner a concrete message to revisit through AAC, pointing, photographs, objects, writing, or partner support. Family knowledge enters the same evidence system and can change vocabulary, sensory planning, materials, routines, or goals. Family response remains optional and can use the mode and timing that works for the family.

## Implementation Sequence

| Time | Team Action | Student Control and Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Before Day 1 | Meet with student/family/SLP; confirm modes, languages, access, interests, vocabulary, privacy, and sensory needs; prepare primary and backup systems; train partners | Student approves priorities, important vocabulary, preferred supports, and what may travel home |
| Days 1-2 | Use the supported routine; confirm positioning, comprehension supports, vocabulary access, and partner wait time | Record which routes the student chooses, which messages are easy/hard, and where breakdowns occur |
| Days 3-5 | Embed communication across observation, evidence comparison, model revision, peer roles, group decisions, and dismissal | Track initiations, message functions, repairs, refusal, role changes, and whether messages affect the activity |
| Day 5 review | Student, family, teacher, SLP, and staff review the first week | Keep, add, move, remove, or revise vocabulary, access routes, sensory supports, partner practices, and home-school fields |
| Days 6-10 | Generalize across different partners and activity moments while preserving predictable communication access | Check whether meaning survives changes in partner, materials, location, and communication route |
| Day 10 decision | Team conducts a joint keep/change/stop review | Student and family participate in deciding the next version of the plan |

The team will never remove the student's primary communication route to create a test condition. Continuity is evaluated through ordinary classroom changes and naturally occurring access needs.

## Data and Decision Rules

The team will collect enough data to make support decisions without turning communication into a compliance score.

| Review Question | Evidence | Revise the Plan When... |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Was communication available? | primary/backup access, positioning, vocabulary, partner availability | the student loses access during transitions, materials, device charging, or staffing changes |
| Did the student have a full message range? | requests, comments, questions, refusal, repair, sensory, academic, and social messages | communication remains concentrated in adult-prompted requests |
| Could the student initiate and redirect? | self-initiated messages, new topics, role changes, corrections | repeated adult prompts replace student initiation |
| Did messages have consequences? | changes in pace, material, role, support, question, sequence, or adult interpretation | partners acknowledge messages while the activity continues unchanged |
| Could breakdowns be repaired? | successful confirmation, repair vocabulary, partner recognition | misunderstandings persist or adults routinely guess the student's meaning |
| Did communication travel? | use across partners, group work, hands-on tasks, dismissal, and home bridge | a route works only with one adult, one device position, or one isolated activity |
| Did agency and privacy increase? | student control of role, refusal, corrections, and outgoing information | adults dominate decisions or share information the student did not approve |

Success is visible when the student has dependable ways to author messages, repair misunderstandings, participate academically and socially, protect boundaries, and influence the environment. The plan is revised when access narrows, partner behavior becomes the barrier, or the student's own feedback points to a better route.

## Evidence Boundary and Revision Commitment

This document is a prospective plan. It describes how I would support one detailed nonspeaking student profile and how I would implement, observe, and revise that support. Camp artifacts provide examples of multimodal participation, sensory feedback, shared-model revision, flexible roles, and evidence-to-action routines. Implementation with an actual student would require the student's and family's knowledge, current AAC assessment, team collaboration, consent, and direct evidence from the classroom.

The plan extends my submitted PTR paper, *From Compliance to Communication*, by giving the learner explicit ways to say *keep this, different order, show me, not yet,* or *you misunderstood* across routines. The central success question remains: after support, can the student communicate more of what matters to them and exercise more influence over learning, relationships, and the environment?
