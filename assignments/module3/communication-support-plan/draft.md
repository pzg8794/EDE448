## Purpose and Scenario

This prospective plan supports a nonspeaking upper-elementary student during an inclusive evidence-to-action science routine and the home-school transition that follows it. It is informed by my EDU486 camp activities, especially model revision, Identity Beads, wearable advocacy, and the final showcase. It does **not** claim that a particular camper was an AAC user. Instead, it applies documented participation routes to a student profile created for this assignment.

The learner uses a speech-generating AAC system with direct touch, gestures, facial expression, and object-based choices. A printed core-and-fringe board provides backup. The student enjoys visual evidence, sorting, photography, and demonstrating with materials. The support goal is not “appropriate device use.” It is reliable authorship across observation, model revision, group decision-making, sensory choices, showcase preparation, and communication with home.

## Framework

The **Puzzle Plan** is my interdisciplinary framework for combining lived experience, education, data, research, technology, and advocacy so that no diagnosis or data point stands alone. **EQUITAS** is its equity-centered heart: it asks whose knowledge is included, what context is missing, how bias and power shape interpretation, and whether support increases dignity, agency, belonging, and access.

This plan therefore measures whether communication support gives the student more ways to influence the routine. That extends my submitted PTR paper, *From Compliance to Communication*, without repeating its behavior plan. The earlier paper showed how an unnecessary either-or condition could be removed. This plan specifies how a learner could ask to keep a familiar tool, reject a material, request a different sequence, comment on evidence, or repair an adult's interpretation.

## Multimodal Communication System

| Component | Purpose | Access Safeguard |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary AAC device | Core language, personalized vocabulary, speech output | Charged, positioned, and available across the routine |
| Printed core/fringe board | Backup and an alternative when the device is unavailable or undesired | Same key vocabulary and consistent symbol locations |
| Gestures, facial expression, body movement | Existing communication modes | Partners notice and confirm rather than requiring device repetition |
| Objects/photos/materials | Referents for evidence, tools, and activity choices | Present concrete choices without limiting all messages to choices |
| Yes/no with repair option | Fast confirmation | Always include “not sure,” “different,” and “I meant something else” |

Mirenda (2015) supports this multimodal approach, and ASHA notes that AAC has no age, cognitive-score, or milestone prerequisites. No mode must be abandoned to prove proficiency with another.

## Vocabulary and Communicative Functions

Core language includes *I, you, it, want, not, more, different, stop, go, help, look, think, like, tell,* and *why*. Activity-specific vocabulary includes *evidence, compare, count, model, label, arrow, water, sample, smell, texture, microscope,* and *showcase*. Repair language includes *wait, show me, say it another way, not that, I meant something else,* and *you misunderstood*.

The system must support more than requesting:

- choosing and refusing;
- commenting and questioning;
- directing a partner;
- expressing sensory preferences or pain;
- predicting, comparing, explaining, and revising;
- joking and social connection;
- asking for help or a pause;
- correcting an interpretation;
- deciding what message travels home.

## Communication Opportunity Matrix

| Routine | Student Can Communicate | Partner Action | Evidence of Access |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Arrival and preview | greet, ask what comes next, choose first role | Review visual sequence; model available words | Student accesses schedule and makes/declines a choice |
| Observe and collect | direct attention, choose tool, comment, reject smell/texture | Keep device/board near materials; offer a non-contact route | Mode, message, and environmental condition are recorded |
| Compare evidence | same/different, more/less, prediction, question | Wait; accept pointing, symbols, photos, or device output | Student contributes evidence rather than only answers prompts |
| Revise model | add/remove, label, arrow, disagree, explain | Give control of materials or a partner-directed role | Student's revision changes the shared model |
| Group decision | vote, pass, propose another option, ask for time | Make passing and write-in choices visible | Decision record includes the student's authored position |
| Showcase | demonstrate, point, answer, ask, hold evidence, partner, observe | Rehearse selected role; do not require public speech | Student selects role and can change it |
| Dismissal/home bridge | choose message, success, barrier, question for home | Co-author the daily record with the student | Student approves or changes what is sent |

Downing, Hanreddy, and Peckham-Hardin (2015) support embedding communication opportunities in ordinary routines and peer interactions. The camp evidence strengthens the design: youth participation already included demonstration, pointing, writing, partnering, observation, pausing, and returning rather than one required performance mode.

## Communication Partner Plan

Adults and peers will keep the system within reach, model language on an adult/partner board, pause long enough for processing, notice all modes, and confirm rather than assume. They will not require the student to repeat a clear gesture on the device, remove AAC as a consequence, physically prompt a message without consent, speak about the student as if absent, or treat refusal as a failed trial.

When a message is unclear, the partner will say what was noticed, offer a way to confirm or repair, and leave room for an unanticipated answer: “I saw you push the sample away. Are you saying *stop*, *different tool*, *need space*, or something else?” The student can answer through any reliable mode.

Peckham-Hardin (2015) emphasizes that a functional communication response must be acknowledged and honored. A break message that never produces a break teaches the learner that the new pathway is unreliable.

## Student-Centered Home-School Record

The supplied Daily Communication Logs offer many formats, but several center adult ratings such as good/fair/poor, compliance, warnings, sitting, or parent initials. I would replace that emphasis with the record below:

| Field | Entry |
| --- | --- |
| Student-authored message to home |  |
| Communication mode(s) used |  |
| Activity and environmental conditions |  |
| What the student chose, refused, asked, commented, or repaired |  |
| Support that increased access |  |
| Barrier that should be changed tomorrow |  |
| Family message or context for school |  |
| Student reviewed/approved the outgoing message | Yes / changed it / chose not to send |

The record follows the student across settings without reducing the day to adult judgments. Family knowledge is evidence, and the student should help decide what information is shared.

## Data, Review, and Consent

For two weeks, the team will sample the selected science routine and dismissal. Data will record: communication opportunities offered; modes used; message functions; partner wait/modeling; breakdowns and repairs; device/board availability; sensory or task conditions; and whether the message changed what happened. The team will review patterns with the student and family, not merely count device activations.

The plan will be revised if it increases prompts without increasing authorship, if the student avoids the system, if partners dominate the interaction, or if messages are acknowledged but not acted on. The success question is the same one developed in *Who Has More Power After Support?*: after support, does the student have more dignity, agency, belonging, access, and power to influence the environment?
