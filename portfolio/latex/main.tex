\section*{Assignment and Design Standard}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Assignment and Design Standard}

This culminating portfolio is worth 40 percent of the EDE448 grade and is due August 7, 2026. The Blackboard prompt contains the internally inconsistent phrase ``at least ten (8) resources/supports.'' I use the more demanding interpretation: ten resources, each designed to stand alone within one or two pages. Every entry explains the support for a teacher or family audience, connects it to communication, social-emotional learning, or behavior, identifies when and why it should be used, and provides relevant sources or websites.

The portfolio reworks applicable Module 1, 2, and 3 assignments into practitioner tools rather than stacking ten old papers together. It uses course readings, public professional guidance, de-identified field evidence, and prospective planning scenarios. Copyrighted course packets and private student records are not reproduced.

\toolsection{Evidence Boundary}

The Pine Brook technology-placement and EDU486 camp examples are de-identified records of practice. They illustrate environmental conditions and participation routes, not diagnoses or universal student traits. The Communication Support Plan is explicitly prospective and does not claim that a particular camper used AAC. The Kit for Kids source is still labeled a simulation draft in the course repository, so this portfolio treats it as a practice-informed lesson design rather than independent proof of delivery. These boundaries are part of the scholarship: evidence is strongest when its limits remain visible.

\section*{Portfolio-Wide Reflection and Revision Commitments}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Portfolio-Wide Reflection and Revision Commitments}

Across these ten resources, the most important change in my thinking is that support cannot be judged only by whether behavior becomes quieter, work becomes faster, or adults experience fewer disruptions. A support is meaningful when the learner has more reliable ways to communicate, participate, refuse, repair, build relationships, demonstrate thinking, and influence what happens next. That standard links sensory design, peer culture, PTR, AAC, classroom routines, and family partnership.

The field evidence also keeps me cautious. One successful change in a technology lesson does not establish a behavioral function. A prospective AAC profile is not a real child's assessment. A checklist does not replace the learner's own communication. A visual routine can become coercive when it is used to force compliance rather than clarify choices. A peer role can become exclusion when adults assign it permanently. For that reason, each resource pairs an implementation tool with a revision trigger.

In future practice, I will review these tools with students, families, special educators, speech-language pathologists, paraprofessionals, and other relevant partners. I will ask whose knowledge is missing, whether the resource works across languages and communication modes, whether the adult actions are feasible, and whether data show greater access rather than only fewer visible concerns. The portfolio is therefore a starting system, not a finished prescription. Its strongest outcome should be more accountable adult design and more student-authored evidence.
