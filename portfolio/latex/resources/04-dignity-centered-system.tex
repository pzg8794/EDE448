\toolsection{What This Resource Is}

This is a classroom-wide support system for an inclusive, technology-rich upper-elementary or middle school setting. It combines predictable instruction, multiple communication routes, sensory access, explicit skill teaching, relationship-based repair, and individualized PTR when universal design is insufficient. Positive behavior support should improve quality of life, social validity, ecological fit, prevention, and stakeholder participation, not simply reduce behavior that adults dislike \citep{dunlap2012pbs}.

The system defines ``positive'' by outcomes: greater dignity, bodily autonomy, communication, self-advocacy, rigorous learning, meaningful choice, regulation without masking pressure, reciprocal relationships, repair, and student/family influence. Gardner's first-hand perspectives add an essential warning: strategies that create shame, trauma, suppression of harmless regulation, or weakened boundaries are not positive merely because a data graph moves downward \citep{gardner2017firsthand}.

\toolsection{Classroom Commitments and Daily Practices}

Post and teach a small set of commitments:

\begin{itemize}
  \item We communicate needs and boundaries.
  \item We make room for different bodies, brains, languages, and communication styles.
  \item We help without taking over.
  \item We use tools, materials, and space safely.
  \item We repair harm and improve the system.
\end{itemize}

Make the commitments observable through daily practices: a visible lesson route; a modeled first step; speech, writing, drawing, pointing, gesture, AAC, dictation, or private response; quiet and movement options; reliable messages such as \textit{help, more time, not yet, stop, break, different tool, disagree,} and \textit{ready to return}; and a reset location that is not punishment. Building communicative competence means making these pathways available all day, not only during therapy or after escalation \citep{jorgensen2018competence}.

\toolsection{A Power-Aware Decision Test}

Before keeping any support, ask:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item What observable barrier or need is this support intended to address?
  \item Whose knowledge shaped the explanation: learner, family, teacher, provider, or only the most powerful adult?
  \item Can the learner accept, refuse, modify, or stop the support?
  \item Does it preserve rigorous learning while changing the access route?
  \item Does it increase meaningful communication and influence, or mainly make the student easier to manage?
  \item What will we monitor about adult implementation and environmental conditions?
\end{enumerate}

Beginning-of-year planning should include chosen names, family languages, communication/AAC, sensory and movement access, health needs, strengths, prior successful supports, and what helps a learner begin, pause, collaborate, and recover. Family knowledge is invited rather than extracted; no student must disclose diagnosis, trauma, poverty, culture, or body history to earn a usable route \citep{zacarian2015community}.

\reflectionbox{EDU486 camp routines showed the value of short explanations, rapid entry into hands-on work, multiple evidence modes, and participant choice. Reflection also exposed system gaps: closure could center speech, not every choice remained visible, and timed work could become a speed test. The lesson plan and adult orchestration therefore belong in behavior-support data; a concern cannot be located only in the learner \citep{campEvidence2026}.}

\adaptationbox{A broad support system can become a poster without practice. Review one routine each week and ask whether all advertised routes were actually available. Too many simultaneous choices can also overwhelm; offer a small, meaningful set and keep a route to ``something else.'' Immediate danger requires calm protective action, but harmless regulation, conflict, overload, and confusion require different responses. Do not collapse them into one discipline category.}
