\toolsection{The Existing Camp Artifact}

The 30-second memo was not a hypothetical exit ticket. During the Invisible Invaders camp, youth were invited on camera after an activity to explain in their own words what they did, how they felt about it, what they learned or confirmed, and what they wanted to investigate next. One surviving Field Friday memo is a 43-second video. Its de-identified transcript records a youth describing the sand-sampling work, identifying it as hands-on, saying they liked it, distinguishing new learning from something confirmed, and connecting the activity to a larger exposure question \citep{campEvidence2026}.

This is a communication support because the learner---not an adult behavior rating---authors the first account of the activity. It is also positive behavioral support: preferences, confusion, sensory responses, questions, and requests for change can become visible before adults reduce them to engagement, compliance, or disruption. The memo gives the next teacher, family member, or collaborator evidence for redesign.

\toolsection{Four Prompts, One Student-Owned Record}

Use the memo after each major activity while keeping the time, mode, and decision to participate flexible.

The facilitator asks once, waits, and follows the learner's language rather than supplying the answer. ``Nothing yet,'' ``I did not like that,'' ``pass,'' and ``delete it'' are valid responses. A memo may be video, audio, AAC, typed text, drawing plus dictated caption, photographs selected by the learner, a partner-supported interview, or no recording.

\toolsection{Ready-to-Reuse Memo Workflow}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Ask permission before recording.} Name the audience and offer a non-camera route.
  \item \textbf{Keep the activity present.} Let the learner hold, point to, photograph, or revisit the work.
  \item \textbf{Use the four prompts as options.} Do not require a polished answer to every question.
  \item \textbf{Let the student review.} Keep, redo, edit, caption, restrict, or delete the memo.
  \item \textbf{Preserve the student's meaning.} Transcribe accurately; retain home language, AAC wording, pauses, and uncertainty.
  \item \textbf{Tag for action, not judgment.} Record the activity, access route, preference or barrier, learning or confirmation, question, and requested change.
  \item \textbf{Close the loop.} Show what changed in the next activity and invite the learner to correct the interpretation.
\end{enumerate}

\toolsection{Puzzle Plan and EQUITAS in Practice}

The Puzzle Plan refuses to let an adult observation, behavior count, photograph, or diagnosis stand alone. The memo adds lived experience and student interpretation to the evidence system. EQUITAS then asks whose account shapes tomorrow's lesson, what the camera could not capture, whether prompting narrowed the answer, and whether the learner can control the record. A memo matters only when youth words can change pacing, materials, access routes, questions, or what adults communicate about them.

\adaptationbox{A camera can create performance pressure or surveillance. Never infer private emotion from appearance, fluency, silence, or refusal. Do not post, send, or retain a memo beyond the agreed audience and purpose. Provide captioning, translation, AAC access, processing time, and a private or asynchronous option. Day 5 had photographs and a same-session transcript but no source video; the evidence system names that gap instead of inventing a memo.}
