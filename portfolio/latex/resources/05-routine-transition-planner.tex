\toolsection{What This Resource Is}

This planner makes the route through a lesson or transition visible without turning the schedule into a demand for rigid compliance. It separates the learning goal from hidden demands involving memory, language, initiation, pacing, materials, public performance, and abrupt stopping. Students can see what is required, what can be chosen, how to ask for change, and how to re-enter after a pause.

Use it before a new routine, a high-load transition, a substitute day, group project, hands-on investigation, digital task, or any pattern where students appear ``not ready'' but the first step, endpoint, communication route, or sensory conditions are unclear.

Pair the route with three pacing labels: \textbf{Must Do} (the smallest evidence needed for the learning goal), \textbf{Can Do} (meaningful extension or another representation), and \textbf{Skip Today} (a component that can wait without hiding consequences). Timers communicate information rather than threats. A familiar tool may travel through a transition when it supports access and safety.

\toolsection{Five-Minute Review After the Routine}

Record which routes were displayed, which were actually usable, what the learner communicated, whether the first step was accessible, where adult prompting increased, and what changed participation. Ask the learner: \textit{What should stay? What should change? What was missing?} The next plan should name one concrete redesign rather than asking the learner to ``try harder.''

\reflectionbox{Across the Pine Brook sensory walk and EDU486 camp, access improved when directions were shorter, a first move was modeled, materials and options were visible, and participation did not depend on one spoken route. Reflection also showed that strong activity design can fail at closure or transition when time pressure narrows choices. The routine itself is therefore evidence, not neutral background \citep{campEvidence2026}.}

\adaptationbox{Visual schedules can become coercive if adults use them only to insist that a student finish. Build in change language: \textit{not yet, different order, show me, more time, skip today,} and \textit{something else}. The goal is predictability with negotiation. For learners who find dense visuals distracting, reduce the display to the current and next step; for learners who need the whole route, keep the endpoint visible.}
