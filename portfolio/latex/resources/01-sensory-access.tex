\toolsection{What This Resource Is}

\noindent
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.70\textwidth}
This is a structured way to examine a classroom from the learner's path rather than from the adult's desk. The reviewer follows one routine from entry through instruction, hands-on work, transition, reset, and exit; records what students must hear, see, touch, filter, remember, and communicate; and redesigns barriers before distress becomes the only visible evidence. The Guggenheim sensory-detective approach and Michigan Developmental Disabilities Institute checklist both direct attention to the complete experience, including noise, lighting, crowds, routes, predictability, and quiet space \citep{guggenheimSensory,michigan2025sensory}.

This resource supports communication and behavior because sensory load can change how quickly a learner processes language, accesses AAC, begins a task, tolerates proximity, or signals a need. Covering ears, moving away, repeating a question, holding onto a familiar tool, or declining a transition may communicate an environmental mismatch rather than defiance.

\toolsection{When and Why to Use It}

Use the walk before opening a classroom, before a noisy or material-heavy lesson, after a schedule or room change, or when participation changes across routines. It is especially useful in technology, robotics, art, science, cafeteria, arrival, dismissal, and other settings where sound, movement, materials, and transitions accumulate. Repeat it at different times; one calm observation cannot represent every condition.

\toolsection{Implementation Sequence}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Select one real routine and define its beginning and end.
  \item Walk the route while the room is in use; record conditions rather than personality labels.
  \item Ask the learner and family which conditions help, hurt, or vary; do not infer preference from behavior alone.
  \item Change one or two high-impact conditions before asking the learner to tolerate more.
  \item Repeat the observation and document whether communication, participation, recovery, or choice increased.
\end{enumerate}

\reflectionbox{\textbf{Practice artifact --- Pine Brook IGNITE sensory walk:} The completed walk rated the room ``sensory-supportive in its teaching practices but not yet fully sensory-designed.'' Picture choice boards, visual steps, pre-sorted materials, movement, and hands-on choices supported entry. During building, Chromebook audio, robot movement, table noise, teacher directions, and cleanup accumulated; no consistently marked low-sensory reset area was visible. One learner joined the blocks activity when a familiar laptop could remain nearby. The observation does not prove why the laptop helped, but it shows that removing an unnecessary either-or condition changed participation and warranted further inquiry.}

\adaptationbox{Sensory-friendliness varies by person, activity, time, pain, fatigue, culture, and prior experience. A checklist cannot prescribe headphones, dim lighting, or breaks for everyone. Reviewers should also avoid creating a reset space that becomes exclusion or a reward contingent on compliance. The revision trigger is simple: if the support reduces visible behavior but also reduces communication, learning, or belonging, redesign it.}
