## Draft Response

One of my main takeaways from Module 2 is that positive support must be judged by what it gives a student, not only by what it stops. Dunlap et al.'s Prevent-Teach-Reinforce model gave me a disciplined way to examine what happens before a behavior, what usable skill or communication route should be taught, and whether the response actually works for the learner. Gardner's first-hand perspectives also made the ethical limit clear: a plan is not fully positive if it reduces visible behavior by increasing masking, shame, trauma, or automatic compliance. Dignity-centered support should increase communication, safety, agency, belonging, and access to learning.

My teaching placement and current EDU486 camp work made that takeaway concrete. When a learner protested the removal of a familiar laptop during a transition to blocks, allowing the laptop and blocks to coexist supported participation that an either/or demand would have hidden. In camp, youth asked for short explanations and one direction at a time; our first field reflection also showed that a rushed closure and timed count could still privilege speech and speed. These experiences remind me to include the adult request, task design, available communication routes, sensory and language demands, and response to support in the evidence. I cannot explain a learner responsibly from one visible moment.

This connects directly to my Puzzle Plan and EQUITAS goals. The Puzzle Plan is my interdisciplinary framework for combining lived experience, education, data, and research to understand complex problems more fully. EQUITAS is the equity-focused lens within that framework, used to examine whose knowledge is included, what context is missing, and where power or bias may shape decisions. In my future classroom, I plan to use visible routines, one direction at a time, AAC and multiple communication routes, sensory and movement options, meaningful role choices, proactive check-ins, and a nonpunitive way to pause and re-enter. I want to explore further how students and families can help define socially meaningful goals and how educators can document outcomes such as self-advocacy, trust, participation, recovery, and belonging alongside behavior-frequency data.
