## Draft Response

I already understand behavioral and social supports as something that should be proactive, relational, and responsive to communication, not limited to what adults do after a student has difficulty. Clear routines, visual information, sensory access, communication supports, meaningful choices, and trusted relationships can prevent confusion from becoming a crisis. I also know that behavior is never the whole explanation. What looks like refusal, disruption, withdrawal, or being "off task" may be connected to an abrupt transition, unclear directions, sensory or language load, fatigue, pain, social risk, an inaccessible tool, or the absence of a reliable way to say "help," "break," "not yet," or "I need a different route."

My teaching placement made this concrete. During a transition from a familiar laptop activity to building with blocks, a learner protested when an adult tried to take the laptop away. I did not require an either/or choice. I allowed the learner to keep the laptop while I invited them into the blocks activity. The learner began building and remained connected. That moment did not tell me exactly why the laptop mattered, but it showed me that changing the environment could reveal participation that forced compliance would have hidden.

My current EDU486 camp work has strengthened the same understanding in a more hands-on setting. Youth told us they needed short explanations, one direction at a time, and a chance to begin quickly. We planned routes that included listening, pointing, drawing, writing, dictating, photographing evidence, working with a partner, passing, and re-entering. Our first field day also showed me where a good plan can break down: time pressure compressed closure, some participation choices were not consistently visible, and a timed count could still feel like a speed test. That is behavioral support too. The teacher has to study whether the learning design is creating the difficulty.

In this module, I hope to explore how Prevent-Teach-Reinforce can help me move from a compassionate instinct to a disciplined process. I want to learn how to define behavior without moral judgment, collect evidence about the environment and adult responses, involve student and family knowledge, teach usable communication or regulation skills, and reinforce agency rather than masking. I am especially interested in how teachers can measure whether a support increases communication, access, safety, belonging, and self-advocacy, not only whether a visible behavior decreases.
