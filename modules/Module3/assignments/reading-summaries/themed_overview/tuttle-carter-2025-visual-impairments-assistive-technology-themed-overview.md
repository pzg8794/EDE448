# Tuttle and Carter (2025): Assistive Technology, Participation, and Belonging

## Source

Tuttle and Carter observed ten secondary students with visual impairments across four local districts and one school for blind students in two U.S. states. The study examined instruction, engagement, and social interaction in core academic classes.

## Findings

Students often participated independently with assistive technology when instructional expectations were clear. Across observations, active engagement averaged 57.2%, passive engagement 12.1%, and nonengagement 30.7%. Written work was the most common expectation, and independent grouping was frequent. Interactions with peers without disabilities appeared in 10.1% of intervals, lower than interactions with general educators at 23.7%.

The study therefore separates academic access from social opportunity. Assistive technology and enrollment in core classes can reveal competence and independence while leaving peer relationships comparatively thin.

## Critical Reading

The sample is small, observations cover one class per student, and the study cannot establish causal effects of assistive technology or instructional arrangements. Percentages describe these observations, not universal student experiences. The social-interaction finding should prompt individualized inquiry rather than assumptions that every student wants the same interaction frequency.

## Puzzle Plan and EQUITAS

This source adds a crucial missing piece: access, independence, and belonging are related but not interchangeable outcomes. EQUITAS asks whether a student can participate in rigorous content and enter relationships on meaningful terms, without defining success as either isolation or forced sociability.

## Assignment Use

- Enrichment source for the journal and takeaways.
- Evidence that the Communication Support Plan needs peer opportunities and student choice, not only device availability and task completion.

## Takeaway

Assistive technology can support independent academic participation, but belonging still requires intentional, consent-respecting social design.
