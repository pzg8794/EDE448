## Meaningful Communication Is Authorship, Not Performance

At the beginning of Module 3, I described assistive technology as a way to
reduce barriers and AAC as a multimodal communication system rather than a
single device. Gesture, gaze, objects, images, text, signs, low-tech boards,
devices, and speech can all belong to that system. Communication access should
not depend on age, a test score, diagnosis, or proving readiness. The module did
not replace that understanding. It made the standard more demanding.

My Module 1 journal, *Who Gets to Name, Interpret, and Design?*, asked how
labels, lived experience, and educational environments shape what the world
recognizes as developmental difference. My Module 2 journal then asked who
gains power after support. Module 3 joins those questions: who is recognized as
the author of a message, which communication is treated as credible, and can
that message alter the adult's interpretation or the environment?

Meaningful communication is not defined by speech, speed, eye contact,
independent device use, English-only performance, or whether a response matches
an adult's expectation. It exists when a student's message is recognized as
theirs, understood or respectfully repaired, and able to influence learning,
relationships, support, and what happens next. A system limited to requesting,
answering teacher questions, or following directions may be technically
functional while withholding language for disagreement, humor, identity,
academic thinking, refusal, and repair.

Norrie, Waller, and Hannah (2021) show the difference between device presence
and communication access. Their findings locate barriers in training, technical
support, staffing, policy, infrastructure, and the fit between technology and
daily life. Downing, Hanreddy, and Peckham-Hardin (2015) move that responsibility
into actual classroom routines, exchanges, peer relationships, and
communication opportunities. A student can be physically present and still
have little access to authorship.

## The Environment Determines Which Competence Becomes Visible

This question is personal for me as a Latino Dominican, bilingual,
neurodivergent, and chronically ill learner and educator. I know how quickly an
access barrier can be rewritten as a character judgment. Fatigue can become
lack of commitment. Processing time can become lack of knowledge. Language
difference can become confusion. A quieter or delayed response can become lack
of interest.

In my K-5 technology teaching placement, participation changed when I reduced
language demands, modeled the exact technology steps, made the sequence visible,
and allowed familiar tools to remain available. In one documented interaction,
a Spanish-language interface did not stop progress because visual demonstration,
pointing, and short directions carried the instruction while the student
completed the task. Language access and partner behavior were part of the
communication system.

EDU486 Invisible Invaders camp made the same principle visible in hands-on
science. Youth could look, move, choose, handle tools, photograph, draw, write,
explain, partner, pause, pass, or return. Identity Beads represented
complementary contributions rather than ranking children by one performance.
During evidence-to-action work and the showcase, youth could revise models,
create advocacy messages, demonstrate, point, answer, ask, hold evidence,
partner, or observe.

## Partner Practice Is a Safety Condition

One de-identified camp incident made adult responsibility impossible to treat as
an abstraction. Across hands-on work, an adult partner used rigid directions,
premature suggestions, and long teacher talk; in one interaction, student work
was undone after being judged incorrect. When I asked the adult privately to
slow down, ask youth what they wanted to do, and hold suggestions until the
students requested or needed them, the adult responded loudly and moved back
toward the group while visibly dysregulated. I redirected the interaction
toward questions and choices so that adult conflict did not become the students'
burden.

The contrast across days was instructive. When the partner used questions and
flexible pacing, participation improved. When rigid direction returned, youth
were slower to respond and less comfortable acting on their own ideas. Quieting,
withdrawal, reluctance to act, or deference after adult pressure could not
fairly be recorded as proof that the students lacked ideas; partner behavior had
changed the safety of communicating them.

Downing et al. (2015) locate communication opportunities inside everyday
exchanges, so adult tone, timing, wait time, and control of materials are part of
the communicative environment. Norrie et al. (2021) show that partner preparation
and organizational support determine whether AAC becomes usable in daily life.
Peckham-Hardin (2015) makes the consequence clear: an alternative message is not
reliable if partners do not honor it. The plan must therefore train adults to
regulate themselves before regulating the activity, ask before suggesting, use
one question or model and then wait, follow a no-touch/no-undo rule, honor
refusal and pass-and-return, and repair after adult behavior reduces safety.

EQUITAS adds that the same adult escalation does not land equally. For Black,
culturally and linguistically diverse, neurodivergent, chronically ill, or
low-speaking youth, race, disability, language, health, and unequal authority
can make public correction, visible frustration, or blocked access to
explanation carry additional threat. A communication plan that leaves adult
power unexamined mislocates the barrier in the child.

## Communication, Behavior, and Reliable Adult Response

Peckham-Hardin (2015) explains that actions adults label challenging may be
connected to a message without a reliable pathway. Functional communication
support investigates purpose, teaches an accessible alternative, and ensures
that partners honor it. A break message that never produces a break teaches the
learner that the new route is less reliable than the action adults are trying
to replace.

This builds on my submitted PTR paper, *From Compliance to Communication*,
without recreating its behavior plan. The original portfolio figure **PTR: From
Evidence to Support** connects that earlier assignment to this journal. Dunlap
et al. (2019) require observable definitions, evidence across contexts, a
provisional hypothesis, linked Prevent-Teach-Reinforce supports, and review.
Peckham-Hardin adds the Module 3 test: the taught route must be accessible and
the message must produce a meaningful response. The figure illustrates that
process; it does not claim a completed functional assessment or prove function.

## AAC Review and Access-to-Agency

My posted *AAC Review: TD Snap* made implementation concrete. TD Snap's page
sets, access routes, editing tools, bilingual Motor Plan options, and printable
low-tech materials create possibilities. Their value depends on feature
matching, personalized vocabulary, backup access, partner practice, and whether
the communicator can do more than request.

In my drafted reply to Avery's TD Snap review, customizable photographs and recurring
subscription costs produced an EQUITAS comparison. Photographs can connect AAC
to familiar people, interests, languages, and identities, but a feature is not
accessible if a school can introduce it and the student or family cannot sustain
it across home, summer, transitions, or a funding change. The team must decide
who will pay, preserve a usable low-tech backup, and protect the student's voice
when the app or speech output is unavailable.

## Puzzle Plan, EQUITAS, and Evidence Justice

The **Puzzle Plan** is my interdisciplinary framework for bringing lived
experience, education, data, research, technology, and advocacy together so no
diagnosis, observation, behavior, or score stands alone. **EQUITAS** is the
equity-centered heart of that framework. It asks whose knowledge is included,
what context is missing, how power and bias shape interpretation, and whether a
support increases dignity, agency, belonging, and access.

Through that lens, communication support is evidence justice. A fair record
documents the student's message, available modes, language, body and
environmental conditions, partner response, breakdowns, repairs, family
knowledge, and response to support. A student-centered home-school record asks
what the learner communicated, what worked, what barrier should change, what
vocabulary is missing, and what the student wants shared.

## Media and Future Practice

In *Wretches and Jabberers*, Tracy Thresher and Larry Bissonnette are autistic
adults with limited speech who had been presumed intellectually incapable and
excluded from ordinary schooling. Access to typing made their existing
authorship harder to dismiss and allowed their messages to change the public
conversation. This connects to Norrie et al. (2021): a device alone does not
create agency. The route must remain usable, partners must treat the message as
consequential, and support must preserve authorship.

In my future classroom, I will treat AAC as a continuously revised multimodal
system rather than one device. Students need vocabulary and routes for choosing,
refusing, commenting, asking, joking, participating academically, expressing
sensory and health needs, protecting privacy, and repairing misunderstandings.
Adults and peers need to model, wait, notice, confirm, and act without requiring
performance or taking over the student's voice.

My central measure remains the one established in my Module 2 journal, *Who Has
More Power After Support?* After communication support is introduced, can the
student author more messages, enter more relationships, challenge an
interpretation, protect a boundary, and influence what happens next? Meaningful
communication looks like a student having something important to say, an
accessible way to say it, and enough power for the message to change the world
around them.
