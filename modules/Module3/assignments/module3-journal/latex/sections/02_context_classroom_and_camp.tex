\reviewsection{The Environment Determines Which Competence Becomes Visible}

This question is personal for me as a Latino Dominican, bilingual, neurodivergent, and chronically ill learner and educator. I know how quickly an access barrier can be rewritten as a character judgment. Fatigue can become lack of commitment. Processing time can become lack of knowledge. Language difference can become confusion. A quieter or delayed response can become lack of interest. Meaningful communication requires adults to examine the conditions under which competence is being judged. In my K--5 technology teaching placement, I saw participation change when I reduced language demands, modeled the exact technology steps, made the sequence visible, and allowed familiar tools to remain available. The learner's competence had not suddenly appeared; the conditions for showing it had changed. In one documented interaction, a Spanish-language interface did not stop progress because visual demonstration, pointing, and short directions carried the instruction while the student completed the task \citep{placementEvidence2026}. Language access and partner behavior were not extras around learning. They were part of the communication system.

My EDU486 Invisible Invaders camp work gives me another concrete picture of this principle. Youth could look, move, choose, handle tools, photograph, draw, write, explain, partner, pause, pass, or return. Identity Beads represented complementary contributions rather than ranking children by one performance. During evidence-to-action work and the showcase, youth could revise models, create advocacy messages, demonstrate, point, answer, ask, hold evidence, partner, or observe \citep{campEvidence2026}. Those routes were not all AAC, and I am not claiming that a particular camper used an AAC system. They do show how classroom design decides which forms of authorship become visible. A communication-rich classroom distributes ways to enter the work before a student is treated as disengaged, incapable, or unwilling.

Figure~\ref{fig:shared-visual-model} is more than a record of a science activity. Read through \citet{downing2015classroom}, it creates a rich communicative environment: the model gives an exchange a visible context while leaving room for an idea, question, uncertainty, revision, or request for help. Read through \citet{mirenda2015aids}, it shows why meaningful communication is multimodal rather than tied to one aid. A learner can point to an existing mark, add a label, redraw a pathway, gesture, speak, write, or use AAC. The partner's responsibility is to notice, confirm, and help the learner revise the shared model without replacing the learner's meaning. This directly answers the journal question because communication becomes consequential when a student's contribution changes the model and the group's next action.

My Module 3 paper, \textit{Communication That Travels With the Student: A Neurodiversity-Affirming, Culturally and Health Responsive Support Plan}, then applied those lessons to the assignment goal of planning instructional and communication strategies for nonspeaking students and explaining how those supports would be implemented \citep{garciabautista2026communicationplan}. Mateo made bilingual and culturally sustaining access visible; Eli made speech and multimodal access visible; and Luca made chronic illness, body state, privacy, low speech, and speech-related trauma visible. Their profiles are fictional, while the support strategies are grounded in de-identified camp and placement evidence. Together, the profiles kept me from designing for an abstract ``AAC student.'' A dependable system must survive changes in language, energy, pain, partner, tool, setting, routine, and willingness to speak.

\reviewsection{Partner Practice Is a Safety Condition}

One de-identified camp incident made adult responsibility impossible to treat as an abstraction. Across hands-on work, an adult partner used rigid directions, premature suggestions, and long teacher talk; in one interaction, student work was undone after being judged incorrect. When I asked the adult privately to slow down, ask youth what they wanted to do, and hold suggestions until the students requested or needed them, the adult responded loudly and moved back toward the group while visibly dysregulated. I redirected the interaction toward questions and choices so that adult conflict did not become the students' burden. The contrast across days was instructive: when the partner used questions and flexible pacing, participation improved; when rigid direction returned, youth were slower to respond and less comfortable acting on their own ideas. Quieting, withdrawal, reluctance to act, or deference after adult pressure could not fairly be recorded as proof that the students lacked ideas; partner behavior had changed the safety of communicating them \citep{campEvidence2026,garciabautista2026communicationplan}.

This incident connects the readings to implementation. \citet{downing2015classroom} locate communication opportunities inside everyday routines and exchanges, so adult tone, timing, wait time, and control of materials are part of the communicative environment. \citet{norrie2021context} show that partner preparation and organizational support help determine whether AAC becomes usable in daily life. \citet{peckhamhardin2015behavior} makes the consequence equally clear: an alternative message is not reliable if partners do not honor it. The Communication Support Plan therefore cannot train only the student. It must train adults to regulate themselves before regulating the activity, ask before suggesting, use one question or model and then wait, follow a no-touch/no-undo rule, honor refusal and pass-and-return, and repair after adult behavior reduces safety.

EQUITAS adds that the same adult escalation does not land equally. For Black, culturally and linguistically diverse, neurodivergent, chronically ill, or low-speaking youth, race, disability, language, health, and unequal authority can make public correction, visible frustration, or blocked access to explanation carry additional threat. I include this incident not to make one adult the whole lesson, but because a communication plan that leaves adult power unexamined mislocates the barrier in the child. Meaningful communication requires enough safety for a student to disagree, refuse, revise, ask for help, and keep ownership of an idea, including when the message requires an adult to change.
