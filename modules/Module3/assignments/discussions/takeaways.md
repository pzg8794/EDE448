# Module 3: Takeaways

## Blackboard Prompt

Which readings did you find useful in this module? What information will you consider in your future practice?

## Ready-To-Submit Response - Not Posted

The three readings I found most useful work together because they move communication support from the device, to the classroom, to the meaning behind a student's actions. Norrie, Waller, and Hannah (2021) showed that high-tech AAC adoption depends on training, technical support, staffing, device fit, and school infrastructure. Their study reinforced that a device placed near a student is not the same as communication access. Downing, Hanreddy, and Peckham-Hardin (2015) helped me see the general education classroom as a communicative environment that must be examined for real opportunities across routines and peer relationships. Peckham-Hardin's (2015) chapter on communication and challenging behavior added that an alternative message becomes reliable only when partners recognize its purpose and honor it. Together, these readings changed my question from whether a student has a device to whether the student can communicate varied messages across real activities and whether those messages influence what happens next.

This connects to what I observed in my teaching placement and EDU486 camp. Participation expanded when I reduced language demands, modeled technology steps, made the sequence visible, and allowed familiar tools and multiple participation routes to remain available. Camp also showed me that access can narrow when an adult uses rigid directions, talks too long, offers ideas before asking what youth want, or makes an irreversible change before students can choose, refuse, revise, or approve it. A student's quieting or hesitation cannot be interpreted responsibly without examining partner behavior and environmental conditions. Communication support must prepare adults, not only students.

In my future practice, I will treat AAC as a continuously revised multimodal system rather than one device. I will keep primary and low-tech backup options available; include language for refusal, commenting, questions, humor, academic ideas, sensory and health needs, and repair; and teach adults and peers to model, wait, confirm meaning, and respond. I will use visible routines, flexible role choices, ask before helping, and keep shared work movable until the student approves a permanent change. I will also redesign communication logs so they preserve the student's message, mode, language, environmental conditions, supports, barriers, successful repairs, family knowledge, and decisions about what may travel between home and school.

The Puzzle Plan is my interdisciplinary framework for bringing lived experience, education, data, research, technology, and advocacy together so that no single diagnosis, observation, behavior, or score becomes the whole learner. EQUITAS is its equity-centered heart: it asks whose knowledge is included, what context is missing, how power and bias shape interpretation, and whether support increases dignity, agency, belonging, and access. Module 3 gives that framework a practical test: after communication support is provided, does the student have more authorship and more power to influence the classroom? If not, the system, partner practice, or environment still needs to change.
