# Communication Support Plan

## Communication That Travels With the Student

### A Neurodiversity-Affirming and Culturally/Linguistically Responsive Plan

**Working draft — not submitted**

## Purpose and Student Design

This plan supports two fictional composite upper-elementary students during inclusive STEM/technology routines and the home-school transition that follows them. Both profiles are neurodivergent. One student is a culturally and linguistically diverse Spanish-English learner whose speech is emerging and not a dependable communication route across settings. The second student is a nonspeaking/multimodal communicator whose participation changes with sensory load, energy, interest, partner behavior, and the structure of the activity.

The two profiles are fictional and do not diagnose or describe any specific child from my EDU486 camp or teaching placement. The instructional moves are grounded in documented practice from those settings: flexible participation routes, interest-based engagement, visual and material supports, wait time, stepwise modeling, sensory choice, pass-and-return options, student-selected roles, bilingual access, learner reflection, and communication that can change the next instructional decision.

The goal is reliable authorship. A strong communication plan gives the learner dependable ways to initiate, ask, refuse, joke, repair, explain, participate academically, choose how to engage, and influence what happens next.

## Student A: Mateo — Neurodivergent, CLD, Spanish-English Emerging Communicator

**Mateo** is a fictional composite student inspired by teaching-placement patterns and support strategies. He comes from a Spanish-speaking family and uses both Spanish and English. His receptive understanding is stronger when directions are visual, demonstrated, broken into steps, or connected to familiar technology and materials. Speech is available in some situations, especially around preferred topics, but it is not reliable enough to be his only communication route. During new, fast, socially demanding, or language-heavy activities, he may communicate more effectively through pointing, gesture, photographs, objects, written or typed words, bilingual visual supports, and AAC.

Mateo is highly motivated by technology, coding, building, visual media, and opportunities to demonstrate competence. His participation increases when adults connect the task to a meaningful goal, allow him to manipulate materials, give him time to process, and avoid treating English production as proof of understanding. His communication system therefore needs to preserve both Spanish and English, support academic as well as social language, and allow him to move between speech and other modes without penalty.

### Mateo's Priority Communication Needs

| Area | Need | Support response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bilingual access | Spanish and English both carry meaning and identity | Provide bilingual vocabulary and permit translanguaging; do not require an English repetition after a clear Spanish message |
| Receptive access | Long spoken directions and unfamiliar interfaces can overload working memory | Pair speech with a first/next/last sequence, modeled examples, screenshots, photographs, and one visible step at a time |
| Reliable expressive routes | Speech varies with task, interest, processing load, and partner expectations | Keep AAC, gesture, pointing, typing, photographs, objects, and speech available together |
| Academic language | Coding, science, and design concepts require specialized vocabulary | Preload or display words such as *code/código, build/construir, test/probar, change/cambiar, evidence/evidencia,* and *help/ayuda* |
| Repair | A language or technology breakdown can leave an adult guess in place | Teach and display *wait/espera, show me/muéstrame, not that/no eso, another way/otra manera,* and *you misunderstood/no entendiste* |
| Social participation | Fast peer talk can narrow participation | Use flexible group roles, visual turn options, partner wait time, and private or asynchronous contribution routes |
| Family continuity | School vocabulary can become disconnected from home language and family knowledge | Invite family vocabulary, preferred terms, interests, and home-language context into the communication system and home-school record |

A teaching-placement transcript documents a technology interface appearing in Spanish while a student was being supported through a stepwise editing task. That moment matters because language access was part of the technology environment itself, not separate from the academic task. The plan therefore treats interface language, partner explanation, visual modeling, and bilingual vocabulary as communication-access conditions.

## Student B: Eli — Neurodivergent Nonspeaking/Multimodal Communicator

**Eli** is a fictional composite student inspired by communication and participation patterns documented during EDU486 camp. Eli communicates through an AAC system, gestures, facial expression, movement, photographs, objects, drawing, and occasional vocalizations. Spoken language is not his dependable communication route. His participation can look very different depending on energy, sensory load, whether the task is meaningful, how much control he has over the role, and whether adults allow time to observe before joining.

Eli is drawn to hands-on investigation, visual evidence, movement, cameras, measuring tools, models, and opportunities to take a real role in a group. A quiet start or low-energy response is treated as information about current access, not evidence of low competence. He may observe first, take a movement break, contribute through a material or visual route, and return later through a named re-entry step.

Camp practice provides concrete examples for this profile. Youth were allowed to identify their current energy without having to justify it; one response of “Just sleepy” was accepted and the activity continued without turning low energy into a behavior problem. Youth also self-selected meaningful roles such as “commentator,” participated through measuring, observing, pointing, writing, speaking, partnering, voting, passing, and returning, and used artifacts such as models and Identity Beads to represent contributions. These strategies become planned communication routes for Eli.

### Eli's Priority Communication Needs

| Area | Need | Support response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Multimodal access | Meaning must survive changes in device, materials, posture, and activity | Keep high-tech AAC, printed backup, gesture, objects, photographs, drawing, and partner-supported access available |
| Processing time | Repeated questions can interrupt formulation | Ask one question, model once, wait at least 10 seconds, and notice self-initiated responses |
| Sensory communication | Smell, texture, noise, crowding, or direct contact can change access | Keep *stop, different, too much, space, observe only, different material,* and *ready later* easy to reach |
| Entry and re-entry | Immediate participation may not be the best route | Offer observe-first, pass, movement/reset, partner, and named return routes |
| Motivation and relevance | Communication increases when the activity has a meaningful purpose | Connect vocabulary to real tools, evidence, choices, roles, problems, and audiences |
| Peer participation | A single oral or public role can exclude competence | Use flexible roles such as evidence finder, recorder, reviser, commentator, demonstrator, photographer, partner, or observer/returner |
| Repair and agency | Adult interpretations need a student-controlled correction route | Keep *not that, wait, different, I meant something else,* and *you misunderstood* available and consequential |

## Why AAC Must Be Multimodal

Mirenda (2015) describes AAC as an evolving combination of aided and unaided techniques. Schlosser et al. (2014) similarly emphasize assessment, feature matching, aided input, communication functions, partners, and generalization. Weng (2016) makes language, family, and community part of AAC access, and ASHA identifies AAC as appropriate without age, cognitive-score, or milestone prerequisites. A communicator should not have to abandon an efficient gesture, home-language word, facial expression, drawing, or object-based message to prove competence with a device.

For both students, the communication system includes:

| Component | Purpose | Access safeguard |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary AAC device | Core language, personalized vocabulary, academic language, social language, and speech output | Available across the full routine, positioned for access, and never removed as a consequence |
| Printed core-and-fringe board | Stable backup during charging, hands-on materials, positioning changes, or student preference | Preserve important vocabulary and predictable locations from the primary system when helpful |
| Spanish-English vocabulary | Identity, family continuity, comprehension, and expressive choice | Student/family help choose vocabulary; both languages remain available without ranking one as more correct |
| Gesture, expression, movement | Existing efficient communication | Partners notice, confirm, and respond without requiring device repetition |
| Objects, photos, models, drawing, typing | Concrete and visual routes for academic and social meaning | Use them for open communication, explanation, questions, and repair as well as choices |
| Shared visual sequence | Comprehension, predictability, and transition support | Show first/next/last, changes, optional routes, and the re-entry point |
| Repair vocabulary | Fast correction after misunderstanding | Keep *wait, not that, show me, another way, you misunderstood,* and *something else* visible in both primary and backup systems |

Norrie, Waller, and Hannah (2021) show that AAC success depends on training, technical support, staffing, coordination, and daily context. A system that works only with one adult or in one specialist session is not enough.

## Communication Functions

The plan supports more than requesting. Both students need communication for:

- greeting and social connection;
- choosing and refusing;
- commenting and questioning;
- directing a partner;
- asking for help, more time, space, or another route;
- expressing pain or sensory boundaries;
- predicting, comparing, explaining, labeling, and revising;
- disagreeing and proposing another idea;
- joking and sharing interests;
- repairing a misunderstanding;
- choosing a peer role or changing roles;
- teaching or demonstrating to another person;
- deciding what information travels home.

Peckham-Hardin (2015) emphasizes that a functional communication response must be acknowledged and honored. A message such as *stop*, *break*, *different*, *no*, or *otra manera* becomes useful when partners respond consistently and the message can affect the environment.

## Communication Opportunity Matrix

Downing, Hanreddy, and Peckham-Hardin (2015) support embedding communication opportunities across ordinary classroom routines and peer interactions. The plan uses the same principle across technology and evidence-to-action STEM work.

| Routine | Mateo can communicate | Eli can communicate | Partner action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Arrival | greet in either language, ask what comes next, choose first task | greet, check schedule, observe first, choose or pass | Review visual sequence; model available vocabulary; protect wait time |
| Technology setup | identify interface language, ask for help, request a demonstration | request device position, choose access route, indicate ready/not ready | Keep communication systems within reach and demonstrate one step at a time |
| Observe | label, photograph, compare, ask, comment | direct attention, choose tool, point, photograph, reject material | Accept speech, AAC, Spanish, English, gesture, objects, and visual evidence |
| Build/revise | explain a change, type a label, direct a partner, ask for clarification | add/remove, label, arrow, disagree, demonstrate, direct a partner | Give students control of meaningful materials and shared products |
| Group decision | vote, explain, write, pass, propose another option | vote, point, pass, write-in, request time, identify an access barrier | Make vote, write-in, pass, and return routes visible |
| Peer role | actor, coder, translator, recorder, debugger, presenter, director | commentator, evidence finder, photographer, reviser, demonstrator, observer/returner | Keep roles flexible and allow students to combine or change them |
| Reflection | record in either language, use photo/voice/text, identify support needed | video/photo memo, point to evidence, choose symbol/object, partner-supported recap | Preserve the student's own meaning before adult interpretation |
| Home bridge | choose bilingual message, question, artifact, or vocabulary for home | choose message, photo, question, contribution, barrier, or privacy option | Student reviews, revises, restricts, or declines what will be shared |

## Flexible Participation and Motivation as Access

Motivation is treated as information about the fit between the learner, task, environment, and available role. It is not used as a character judgment. If a student communicates more around technology, movement, photography, measuring tools, visual evidence, building, or public advocacy, those interests become legitimate entry points into academic communication.

Camp documentation supports several concrete practices:

1. **Accept the student's current state.** A low-energy response can be acknowledged without converting it into a compliance problem.
2. **Offer a meaningful role.** Youth could self-select roles such as commentator and participate through real intellectual work.
3. **Keep multiple routes open.** Speaking, writing, pointing, demonstrating, photographing, partnering, observing, voting, passing, and returning all remained valid.
4. **Make evidence visible.** Models, labels, arrows, measurements, photographs, and artifacts gave students something concrete to communicate about.
5. **Let communication change the plan.** Youth suggestions shaped models, policy discussion, advocacy messages, and future activity decisions.

These practices become explicit supports for Mateo and Eli rather than informal accommodations that depend on a particular adult remembering them.

## Communication Partner Plan

Adults and peers will:

- keep the student's communication system within reach across the routine;
- model language without demanding imitation;
- ask one question and protect processing time;
- recognize Spanish, English, AAC, gesture, facial expression, movement, pointing, objects, photographs, writing, typing, and drawing as communication;
- confirm meaning when a message is unclear;
- ask before touching a device, body, materials, or workspace;
- return control immediately after assistance;
- address the student directly;
- avoid translating or correcting a bilingual message when the meaning is already clear;
- honor refusal, correction, disagreement, pain, sensory boundaries, and requests for another route;
- record partner and environmental barriers alongside student communication data.

When meaning is unclear, the partner can describe what was observed and offer a repair route: “I saw you move the sample away. Are you saying *stop*, *different tool*, *need space*, *otra manera*, or something else?” The student can confirm or repair through any reliable mode.

## Peer Plan: Invite, Wait, Ask, Respect

Peers learn four actions: **invite, wait, ask, and respect**.

- **Invite:** make a real role available without deciding the student's role for them.
- **Wait:** protect processing time and do not fill silence automatically.
- **Ask:** ask before helping, moving materials, translating, speaking for the student, or touching AAC.
- **Respect:** accept no, a role change, a different language, a quieter route, a pass, or a later return.

Possible roles include starter/modeler, evidence finder, recorder/mapper, connector/questioner, reviser/debugger, materials/access lead, commentator, photographer, presenter/demonstrator, translator when voluntarily chosen, and observer/returner. Every role carries meaningful work and can change during the activity.

## Student-Centered Bilingual Home-School Record

The supplied *Daily Communication Logs* packet demonstrates many school-home formats, including ratings, checklists, teacher comments, family comments, and parent initials. Several examples center adult judgments such as good/fair/poor days, warnings, sitting, and compliance (EDE 448 Course Materials, 2009). This plan uses a record that carries student meaning, language, context, and family knowledge across settings.

| Field | Entry |
| --- | --- |
| Student-authored or student-approved message to home |  |
| Language(s) and communication mode(s) used |  |
| Activity and environmental conditions |  |
| What the student chose, refused, asked, commented, taught, or repaired |  |
| Support that increased access |  |
| Barrier or condition to change tomorrow |  |
| Question or unfinished idea |  |
| New vocabulary needed in Spanish, English, or both |  |
| Family message, vocabulary, context, or question for school |  |
| Student reviewed outgoing message | approved / changed / restricted / chose not to send |

Family knowledge can change vocabulary, materials, sensory planning, examples, routines, and goals. Families can respond in Spanish, English, another language, photographs, audio, written notes, or another accessible form. The student helps decide what information travels between settings.

## Implementation Sequence

| Time | Team action | Student control/evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Before Day 1 | Meet with student, family, SLP, and teacher; confirm modes, languages, access, interests, vocabulary, privacy, sensory needs, and preferred roles | Student/family identify important vocabulary, language preferences, supports, and what may travel home |
| Days 1-2 | Establish primary and backup systems; use visual sequence; confirm bilingual vocabulary and positioning; train partners | Record which routes each student chooses and where breakdowns occur |
| Days 3-5 | Embed communication in observation, technology use, building, peer roles, group decisions, reflection, and dismissal | Track initiations, questions, comments, refusals, repairs, role changes, language choices, and whether messages affect the activity |
| Day 5 review | Student, family, teacher, SLP, and staff conduct a keep/change/add/remove review | Student and family change vocabulary, routes, sensory supports, partner practices, or home-school fields |
| Days 6-10 | Generalize across partners, materials, locations, and academic tasks | Check whether meaning survives changes in partner, language demand, tool, and setting |
| Day 10 decision | Joint review of access, message range, agency, and implementation | Student and family participate in deciding the next version of the plan |

The team will not remove the student's primary communication route to create a test condition. Communication continuity is examined through ordinary classroom variation.

## Data and Decision Rules

| Review question | Evidence | Revise when... |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Was communication available? | primary/backup access, positioning, bilingual vocabulary, partner availability | access disappears during transitions, hands-on materials, language changes, device charging, or staffing changes |
| Was the message range broad enough? | comments, questions, refusal, repair, sensory, academic, social, humor, and home-language messages | communication remains concentrated in adult-prompted requests |
| Could the student initiate and redirect? | self-initiated messages, new topics, role changes, corrections, language choice | repeated prompting replaces student initiation |
| Did messages have consequences? | changes in pace, tool, material, language, role, support, question, sequence, or adult interpretation | partners acknowledge a message while the activity continues unchanged |
| Could breakdowns be repaired? | successful confirmation, bilingual repair vocabulary, partner recognition | adults routinely guess or misunderstand without giving the student a correction route |
| Did communication travel? | use across partners, technology, hands-on work, peers, dismissal, and home bridge | a route works only with one adult, one language, one device position, or one activity |
| Did agency and belonging increase? | student control of language, role, refusal, correction, privacy, and contribution | support makes the student more passive or gives adults more control over communication |

## Evidence Boundary and Practice Connection

The student profiles are fictional composites created for the assignment. The teaching-placement and camp records supply documented instructional strategies and participation conditions, not diagnoses. The plan carries those tested teaching moves into a communication profile that meets the assignment's focus on nonspeaking and emerging communicators.

The teaching placement contributes bilingual/interface access, stepwise modeling, technology-rich multimodal work, visual demonstrations, peer collaboration, and personalized support. EDU486 contributes flexible participation routes, interest-based roles, low-pressure entry and return, multimodal reflection, youth-selected identity/contribution artifacts, sensory choices, and evidence that student messages can shape a shared product or next decision.

The Puzzle Plan keeps these pieces connected: student communication, culture and language, environment, materials, family knowledge, partner behavior, and observed outcomes. EQUITAS asks who controls interpretation and whether the support expands dignity, agency, belonging, and access. The central success question is simple: **after support, can the student communicate more of what matters to them and exercise more influence over learning, relationships, and the environment?**
