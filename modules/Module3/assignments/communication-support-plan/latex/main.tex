\reviewsection{Purpose and Student Design}

This plan supports three upper-elementary fictional pooled-composite students during inclusive STEM and Computer Science routines and the home--school transition that follows them. Mateo foregrounds neurodivergence, culture, and language; Eli foregrounds neurodivergence and speech/multimodal access; and Luca foregrounds neurodivergence, chronic illness, low speech, and speech-related trauma. These are planning lenses, not sealed categories: any learner may need the language, health, sensory, technology, pacing, role, or partner supports foregrounded elsewhere. No observed child maps one-to-one to any profile, and a documented experience from any learner may inform any profile when the access condition fits. Grounded in de-identified EDU486 camp evidence, K--5 teaching-placement evidence, and prior EDE448 work, this design examines distinct and intersecting needs while preserving one principle: speech should not be the price of participation. The goal is reliable authorship---ways to initiate, refuse, repair, ask, comment, joke, explain, choose a role, protect sensory and body boundaries, influence the activity, and decide what information travels home.

\reviewsection{Adult Partner Practice as Communication Access}

On showcase day, I arrived about one hour late because of my health. That matters: my lateness was a team-context variable and could reasonably have affected my partner's confidence. I had nevertheless prepared a data-organization mini-lesson with images and slides classified by day and activity. The instructional purpose was to make sequence and patterns visible, then invite youth to decide which evidence belonged on the trifold, how it should be arranged, and what message should occupy the center. Camp records separately preserve my description of the data as organized by day and activity and document that youth used the trifold, photographs, model, samples, and tools to tell an evolving investigation story \citep{campEvidence2026}.

In my de-identified reflective account, my partner praised the organized set but, before asking its purpose or consulting youth, removed the sequence, mixed the materials, selected a center, and glued the arrangement. When I asked what the change was meant to teach and why it needed to replace the planned choice process, I could not identify a learning reason that justified making the decision permanent. I stepped back so adult disagreement would not become the activity, then intervened when the same directive pattern moved toward students. This analysis does not claim a motive. It examines observable decisions and their consequences: a reversible, student-authored data task became an irreversible, adult-authored product before youth could choose, refuse, revise, or approve it.

The organized display was not merely decoration; it was a shared visual communication support through which a learner could point, order, question, compare, repair, and explain. Course readings describe the classroom environment, visual/aided routes, and partner coordination as parts of communication access \citep{downing2015classroom,mirenda2015aids,norrie2021context}. This incident also extends the evidence-first standard in my submitted PTR paper and the power test in my Module 2 journal: support must increase authorship, not merely produce a polished artifact \citep{garciabautista2026ptr,garciabautista2026module2journal}. The Puzzle Plan keeps my health, preparation, artifact sequence, partner action, youth options, and outcome visible as different pieces. EQUITAS asks who controlled the center, order, permanence, and interpretation. Fairness requires both accountability for my late arrival and a safeguard against uncertainty becoming unilateral control over student meaning.

\reviewsection{Communication Must Cover the Full Message Range}

All three systems support greeting, social connection, choosing, refusing, commenting, questioning, directing a partner, expressing pain or sensory boundaries, predicting, comparing, explaining, labeling, revising, disagreeing, joking, asking for help or more time, repairing misunderstandings, choosing or changing a peer role, teaching another person, and deciding what information travels home. \citet{peckhamhardin2015behavior} connect functional communication with meaningful outcomes. A message becomes dependable when the partner response shows the student that communication can influence the environment.

\reviewsection{Communication Partner Plan}

Adults and peers will keep each communication system within reach, model language on a partner display, ask one question at a time, protect processing time, notice all reliable modes, confirm unclear meaning, ask before helping, return control after assistance, address the student directly, honor language choice, and respond to refusal, correction, disagreement, pain, sensory boundaries, or a request for another route. Mateo's profile foregrounds bilingual repair, Eli's multimodal recognition, and Luca's health/privacy access, but any of those supports can travel to another profile when the condition fits. Before changing a shared artifact, partners follow \textsc{purpose--ask--keep movable--student approves--make permanent}. When meaning is unclear, a partner can say: ``I saw you move the sample away. Are you saying \textit{stop}, \textit{different tool}, \textit{need space}, \textit{otra manera}, or something else?'' The student can verify via any reliable mode.

\reviewsection{Flexible Peer Roles and Participation Routes}

Peers learn four actions: \textsc{invite, wait, ask, and respect}. They invite a real role, wait for the student's response, ask before helping or speaking for someone, and respect a role change, different language, quieter route, pass, health break, or later return. Possible roles include starter/modeler, coder, evidence finder, recorder/mapper, connector/questioner, reviser/debugger, materials/access lead, commentator, photographer, presenter/demonstrator, translator when voluntarily chosen, and observer/returner. Every role carries meaningful intellectual work and can change during the activity.

\normalsize

\citet{norrie2021context} show that AAC adoption depends on training, technical support, staffing, coordination, and daily context. Responsibility therefore sits across the team: the teacher prepares communication opportunities, the SLP supports access and vocabulary organization, classroom staff maintain high- and low-tech availability, the family contributes language, health, and context, and the student directs priorities through ongoing feedback.

The central success question is the same one that runs through my Puzzle Plan and EQUITAS work: after support, can the student communicate more of what matters to them and exercise more influence over learning, relationships, and the environment? Communication support succeeds when the student's meaning survives changes in language, tool, partner, place, body state, and routine and remains powerful enough to shape what happens next.

\reviewsection{Practice and Evidence Boundary}

Mateo, Eli, and Luca are fictional pooled composites. Their identities and support profiles were designed for this assignment; none corresponds to one real learner. The shared evidence bank contributes observable strategies and participation patterns, and any pattern may inform any profile when the access condition fits. In the showcase account, camp records document data organized by day/activity and youth use of the trifold, photographs, model, samples, and tools; my de-identified practitioner reflection contributes the sequence of partner actions and my interpretation of their instructional consequences. Those evidence types are related but not interchangeable. I do not infer the partner's motive, diagnose a learner, or claim that an observed child was nonspeaking or used AAC. The purpose is to convert a difficult teaching event into specific safeguards for access, student authorship, adult accountability, and repair.
