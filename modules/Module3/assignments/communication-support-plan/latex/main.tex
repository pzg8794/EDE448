\reviewsection{Purpose and Student Design}

This plan supports two nonspeaking upper-elementary learners during inclusive STEM and technology routines and the home--school transition that follows them. Both students are neurodivergent. Mateo is a culturally and linguistically diverse Spanish--English learner who communicates through a bilingual AAC system, typing, photographs, gesture, pointing, objects, and selected written words. Eli has a fictional motor-speech disability that makes speech production effortful and unreliable; he communicates through AAC, gesture, facial expression, movement, photographs, objects, drawing, and occasional vocal approximations. Each learner needs a full communication system that supports academic ideas, social connection, refusal, repair, sensory communication, peer participation, and influence over what happens next.

The assignment permits a plan for a student or students who are nonspeaking, which makes a two-student design appropriate for examining how communication support changes across language, sensory access, motivation, partner behavior, and preferred activity routes. The plan extends my earlier EDE448 work on access and agency \citep{garciabautista2026ptr,garciabautista2026module2journal} and uses the portfolio strategies on AAC selection, low-tech continuity, flexible peer roles, and student-centered evidence as implementation tools.

\reviewsection{Student A: Mateo -- Neurodivergent CLD Spanish--English Communicator}

Mateo is a fictional neurodivergent CLD student whose primary home language is Spanish and whose school environment uses English. He is nonspeaking and communicates through a bilingual speech-generating AAC system, typing, photographs, pointing, gesture, and familiar written words. His receptive access improves when instructions are demonstrated, visually sequenced, connected to a concrete goal, and presented one step at a time. Technology, coding, building, visual media, and opportunities to demonstrate competence are strong entry points for communication.

Teaching-placement evidence directly informs this profile. In one documented technology lesson, a student interface appeared in Spanish during stepwise editing support. The interaction continued through visual demonstration, pointing, and short directions; the student completed the editing step and the group moved into successful production work during the same session \citep{placementEvidence2026}. The plan carries that practice forward through bilingual vocabulary, interface-language awareness, visual modeling, and flexible response routes.

\reviewsection{Student B: Eli -- Neurodivergent Nonspeaking Multimodal Communicator}

Eli is a fictional neurodivergent student with a motor-speech disability. His speech output is infrequent and requires substantial effort, so AAC and other multimodal routes carry most of his classroom communication. His participation changes with sensory load, energy, task meaning, partner pace, and the amount of control available in the activity. Hands-on investigation, movement, cameras, measuring tools, models, and meaningful group roles create strong communication entry points.

EDU486 camp documentation contributes the central strategies for this profile. Youth could name their current energy and have it accepted as useful information; one youth's ``Just sleepy'' response was followed by continued access to the activity. Youth also selected meaningful roles such as commentator and participated through measuring, observing, writing, pointing, photographing, partnering, voting, passing, and returning. Their contributions changed shared models, policy discussions, advocacy messages, and reflection products \citep{campEvidence2026}. Eli's plan turns those successful participation routes into predictable communication supports.

\reviewsection{A Multimodal and Bilingual Communication System}

\citet{mirenda2015aids} describes AAC as a continuously updated combination of aided and unaided techniques. ASHA also identifies AAC as appropriate across ages and support profiles \citep{ashaAAC}. \citet{schlosser2014aac} emphasize communication functions, partners, settings, maintenance, and generalization, while \citet{weng2016language} makes the learner's language, family, and community part of feature matching. These principles support a system that travels through the student's real day.

\reviewsection{Communication Must Cover the Full Message Range}

Both systems support greeting, social connection, choosing, refusing, commenting, questioning, directing a partner, expressing pain or sensory boundaries, predicting, comparing, explaining, labeling, revising, disagreeing, joking, asking for help or more time, repairing misunderstandings, choosing or changing a peer role, teaching another person, and deciding what information travels home. \citet{peckhamhardin2015behavior} connect functional communication with meaningful outcomes. A message becomes dependable when the partner response shows the student that communication can influence the environment.

\reviewsection{Communication Partner Plan}

Adults and peers will keep each communication system within reach, model language on a partner display, ask one question at a time, protect processing time, notice all reliable modes, confirm unclear meaning, ask before helping, return control after assistance, address the student directly, honor language choice, and respond when the student communicates refusal, correction, disagreement, pain, sensory boundaries, or a request for another route.

For Mateo, partners treat Spanish and English as usable communication resources and keep bilingual repair vocabulary visible. For Eli, partners watch for gesture, body movement, objects, photographs, drawing, and AAC as part of the same message system. When meaning is unclear, a partner can describe the observed action and offer a repair route: ``I saw you move the sample away. Are you saying \textit{stop}, \textit{different tool}, \textit{need space}, \textit{otra manera}, or something else?'' The student can confirm through any reliable mode.

\reviewsection{Flexible Peer Roles and Participation Routes}

Peers learn four actions: \textbf{invite, wait, ask, and respect}. They invite a real role, wait for the student's response, ask before helping or speaking for someone, and respect a role change, different language, quieter route, pass, or later return. Possible roles include starter/modeler, coder, evidence finder, recorder/mapper, connector/questioner, reviser/debugger, materials/access lead, commentator, photographer, presenter/demonstrator, translator when voluntarily chosen, and observer/returner. Every role carries meaningful intellectual work and can change during the activity.

\citet{norrie2021context} show that AAC adoption depends on training, technical support, staffing, coordination, and daily context. Responsibility therefore sits across the team: the teacher prepares communication opportunities, the SLP supports access and vocabulary organization, classroom staff maintain high- and low-tech availability, the family contributes language and context, and the student directs priorities through ongoing feedback.

\reviewsection{Data, Review, and Success}

The team collects enough evidence to change support while keeping the student's communication at the center. The review focuses on availability, message range, initiation, repair, partner response, language access, sensory access, and whether communication produces a meaningful consequence.

The central success question is the same one that runs through my Puzzle Plan and EQUITAS work: after support, can the student communicate more of what matters to them and exercise more influence over learning, relationships, and the environment? Communication support succeeds when the student's meaning survives changes in language, tool, partner, place, and routine and remains powerful enough to shape what happens next.

\reviewsection{Practice and Evidence Boundary}

Mateo and Eli are fictional composite students. Their profiles combine the assignment's nonspeaking-student requirement with documented support strategies from the EDU486 camp and K--5 teaching placement. The source records contribute observable teaching moves and participation evidence; the fictional profiles provide the neurodivergent, CLD, bilingual, and motor-speech characteristics needed to examine how those strategies can be implemented in a communication support plan.
